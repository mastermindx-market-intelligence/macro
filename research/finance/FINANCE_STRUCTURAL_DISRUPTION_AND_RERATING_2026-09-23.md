## 1. Executive ruling

### R-FIN-DIS-1 — Disruption is a staged economic process, not a headline

Every disruption claim must preserve the full ladder:

```text
technical capability
→ legal / regulatory permission
→ operational integration
→ distribution and customer access
→ adoption / activity
→ monetization
→ margin / loss / capital effect
→ per-share earnings or book-value effect
→ expectations and valuation recognition
```

A firm may have technical capability without legal permission, permission without production readiness, production without meaningful use, use without revenue, or revenue without attractive per-share economics.

### R-FIN-DIS-2 — Realized, contracted, enabled and optionality states remain distinct

Closed initial states:

- `TECHNICAL_DEMONSTRATION`;
- `REGULATORY_PROPOSAL`;
- `TEMPORARY_OR_CONDITIONAL_RELIEF`;
- `APPROVED_OR_LICENSED`;
- `OPERATIONALLY_CONNECTED`;
- `LIMITED_PRODUCTION`;
- `COMMERCIALLY_AVAILABLE`;
- `REPORTED_ADOPTION`;
- `REPORTED_REVENUE`;
- `REPORTED_EARNINGS_MATERIALITY`;
- `VALUATION_OPTIONALITY_ONLY`;
- `DISCONTINUED_OR_SUPERSEDED`.

The product may not promote a lower state into a higher state by implication.

### R-FIN-DIS-3 — Regulation can create both opportunity and cost

A rule or law can:

- open a new permissible activity;
- clarify ownership or liability;
- increase trust and institutional adoption;
- create licensing or compliance barriers;
- change capital/liquidity requirements;
- constrain product economics;
- favor incumbents with compliance, data, distribution and balance-sheet scale;
- create challengers with a lower-cost architecture;
- delay adoption through implementation uncertainty.

“Regulatory clarity” is not automatically bullish for every participant.

### R-FIN-DIS-4 — Infrastructure modernization can strengthen incumbents

Tokenization, instant payments, APIs, cloud and AI do not necessarily eliminate established financial infrastructure. Incumbent utilities, custodians, networks, data providers, banks, brokers and software firms may preserve or expand their roles by supplying identity, permissioning, custody, settlement, reporting, compliance and interoperability.

Disruption analysis must map:

```text
incumbent role
challenger role
new enabling role
activity displaced
activity preserved
new revenue pool
margin/capital effect
customer-switching path
```

### R-FIN-DIS-5 — Lower unit cost can expand the market while compressing take rate

Examples include:

- instant or stablecoin payment rails;
- automated advice;
- tokenized issuance/settlement;
- AI-based underwriting or servicing;
- direct indexing;
- private-market distribution;
- cloud/core modernization.

Volume growth and revenue growth may diverge if pricing declines faster than adoption rises.

### R-FIN-DIS-6 — Autonomous finance increases the value of identity, permission and control

Agentic systems may initiate research, advice, payments, transfers, applications, claims, trading instructions or service actions. That increases demand for:

- identity and delegated authority;
- consent and revocation;
- transaction limits;
- auditability;
- model/data lineage;
- fraud and anomaly detection;
- suitability/best-interest controls;
- human escalation;
- dispute and error resolution.

The valuable layer may be the governed control plane rather than the visible agent interface.

### R-FIN-DIS-7 — Cyber and fraud are an economic counterweight to digitization

Faster, more open and more automated systems can increase:

- credential theft;
- account takeover;
- synthetic identity;
- authorized-push-payment fraud;
- deepfake/social-engineering fraud;
- model manipulation;
- data leakage;
- third-party concentration;
- operational-resilience risk.

Every digital-adoption thesis must include fraud, reimbursement, loss, compliance and customer-trust economics.

### R-FIN-DIS-8 — The same disruption can create winners and losers inside one issuer

A diversified financial company may:

- lose legacy transaction or custody fees;
- gain software, data, collateral or distribution revenue;
- reduce operating cost;
- incur higher compliance, fraud or integration expense;
- release capital;
- increase operational concentration;
- cannibalize an existing business.

Issuer-level net impact requires a business-line bridge, not logo-level theme membership.

### R-FIN-DIS-9 — Optionality requires probability and dilution discipline

Disruption optionality must preserve:

- current investment and operating cost;
- likely commercialization clock;
- addressable revenue pool;
- adoption probabilities;
- take rate and margin;
- competitive share;
- capital and regulatory burden;
- cannibalization;
- dilution/acquisition cost;
- terminal assumptions.

A broad future market does not justify any current valuation.

### R-FIN-DIS-10 — No direct trade authority

Disruption research remains descriptive, evaluative and source-backed. It does not originate, rank, gate, size or execute trades.

## 2. Current regulatory and infrastructure boundary — September 2026

### 2.1 Payment stablecoins

The United States enacted the GENIUS Act in July 2025. As of this research date:

- Treasury, OCC and FDIC have issued multiple proposed implementing rules;
- regulatory proposals cover issuer licensing, reserve assets, redemption, capital, risk management, custody/safekeeping, reporting, AML/CFT and sanctions;
- Treasury stated in its August 2026 proposed rule that the expected statutory effective date is 18 January 2027;
- final regulations and exact commercial conditions remain an implementation dependency;
- FDIC proposals distinguish payment stablecoins from tokenized deposits and state that technology does not change whether a qualifying bank liability is a deposit;
- proposed reserve and redemption rules can create asset-management, custody, compliance, reporting and bank-partnership economics while constraining yield and balance-sheet uses.

The investable question is not simply “stablecoins are legalized.” It is:

```text
who may issue
what reserves are permitted
who owns reserve yield
what redemption/liquidity/capital is required
who holds custody and keys
which rail/customer/workflow adopts
what fraud/compliance cost arises
which legacy revenue is displaced
what per-share economics remain
```

### 2.2 Tokenized securities

The SEC staff described tokenized securities in January 2026 as securities represented through crypto-network records, preserving the distinction between issuer-sponsored and third-party tokenization models.

On 17 September 2026, the SEC issued temporary, conditional exemptive relief for certain Tokenized Securities Venues to support limited trading of tokenized NMS stock through permissioned liquidity pools. This is an important legal and market-structure development, but it is:

- temporary and conditional;
- limited to specified venues, participants, assets and protections;
- not evidence that all securities trading has moved onchain;
- not evidence of material revenue for every exchange, broker, custodian, wallet, blockchain or market maker.

### 2.3 DTC tokenization and collateral

DTC received SEC no-action relief in December 2025 for a defined tokenization service covering specified DTC-custodied assets.

DTCC reported:

- limited production tokenized transactions in July 2026 across collateral pledge, securities lending, Treasury/repo DVP, equity DVP/DVD, token transfer and CCP margin use cases;
- an announced October 2026 target for broad service launch;
- a tokenized collateral platform expected to go live in the fourth quarter of 2026;
- Fund/SERV admission of its first tokenization platform member in September 2026, connecting tokenized funds to an infrastructure serving most U.S. mutual-fund transaction activity.

These facts support an `OPERATIONALLY_CONNECTED` / `LIMITED_PRODUCTION` state for defined use cases—not universal commercial adoption or material earnings.

### 2.4 Open banking / personal financial data rights

The CFPB’s personal financial data-rights rule remains legally and operationally unsettled. The CFPB states that the compliance dates were stayed by a court on 29 October 2025 and that amendments/compliance-date changes have been under consideration.

Therefore, a U.S. open-banking investment thesis must separate:

- statutory data-right principle;
- current rule text;
- court stay;
- amendment/proposal state;
- voluntary API/bank-network adoption;
- commercial contracts;
- data-aggregator economics;
- liability, fraud and consent.

### 2.5 Instant payments

FedNow launched in July 2023. Federal Reserve reporting showed 1,192 participating institutions and 1.5 million transactions in 2024, with adoption expected to grow from a low initial base.

By contrast, Federal Reserve commercial ACH processed 5.577 billion items worth $12.814 trillion in 2026Q2. This scale difference demonstrates why a new rail can be strategically important before it is economically material.

Instant-payment research must preserve:

- reachable institutions/accounts;
- receive-only versus send capability;
- actual transaction volume/value;
- use case;
- fraud/error handling;
- pricing;
- treasury/software integration;
- liquidity and 24/7 operations;
- displacement versus incremental volume.

### 2.6 AI governance in banking and insurance

The bank regulators’ April 2026 revised model-risk guidance expressly excluded generative and agentic AI from scope and indicated that additional work on AI-based models was planned. This creates a moving governance boundary rather than blanket approval.

NAIC’s AI framework includes a 2023 model bulletin and ongoing 2025–2026 work on regulator evaluation tools. As of March 2026, NAIC described an AI Systems Evaluation Tool pilot involving 12 states.

FINRA’s 2026 oversight report described member-firm GenAI adoption as focused mainly on internal efficiency, especially summarization and information extraction.

These sources support a staged AI-adoption model:

```text
internal productivity
→ supervised workflow assistance
→ customer-facing recommendation/service
→ autonomous or decision-bearing action
```

The control, liability and evidence requirements rise sharply at each stage.

## 3. Stablecoin economic map

### 3.1 Workflow

```text
fiat funding source
→ issuer / licensed issuing entity
→ reserve manager and reserve assets
→ reserve custodian / bank
→ mint / burn and ledger
→ wallet / exchange / payment provider
→ merchant / institution / user
→ redemption / off-ramp
→ reporting / compliance / audit
```

### 3.2 Revenue pools

Potential pools:

- reserve income;
- issuance/redemption fees;
- wallet/custody fees;
- payment-processing or merchant fees;
- FX and cross-border spread;
- treasury/cash-management software;
- distribution and embedded-finance fees;
- compliance/identity/analytics;
- blockchain transaction/sequencing economics;
- bank deposit/custody relationships.

### 3.3 Required distinctions

- payment stablecoin versus tokenized deposit;
- issuer versus reserve custodian/manager;
- circulating supply versus transaction volume;
- onchain transfer versus commercial payment;
- gross reserve yield versus issuer net revenue;
- primary issuance/redemption versus secondary trading;
- wallet balance versus insured bank deposit;
- domestic versus cross-border use;
- regulated/approved versus offshore/unlicensed activity.

### 3.4 Rerating conditions

A listed company can rerate on stablecoin exposure only when evidence shows one or more of:

- license/approval and operational readiness;
- material active supply or transaction volume;
- identifiable take rate or reserve economics;
- customer/merchant/institution adoption;
- custody or distribution revenue;
- cross-sell into treasury, data, compliance or payments;
- improved working-capital or settlement economics;
- durable margin after compliance, fraud and incentive cost;
- per-share earnings materiality.

### 3.5 De-rating/falsifiers

- supply growth is speculative rather than transactional;
- reserve economics fall with rates;
- regulation constrains yield or permissible activity;
- redemption/liquidity cost rises;
- fraud, AML/sanctions or cyber cost escalates;
- distribution depends on subsidies;
- network fragmentation limits interoperability;
- tokenized deposits or bank rails displace the issuer;
- current valuation capitalizes an earnings pool not yet reported.

- greater advisor capacity;
- improved fraud/model detection;
- personalized pricing or advice;
- new data/software revenue;

Potential costs/risks:

- privacy and intellectual-property exposure;
- cyber and agent authorization risk;
- compliance and human-review cost;
- reputational/customer remediation;

### 7.3 Beneficiary map

**Incumbents with proprietary workflow/data**

- credit bureaus and ratings/data providers;
- payment/fraud/identity networks;
- insurance forms/loss-cost/claims-data providers;
- custodians, brokers and banks with customer/context data;
- core, risk and compliance software.

**Enablers**

- cloud, model, observability and security infrastructure;

**Potentially disrupted**

- manual processing and call-center work;
- undifferentiated research/reporting;
- low-value advisory/administration;
- legacy rules engines without data/workflow advantages;

### 7.4 Rerating conditions

- disclosed production deployment, not pilot count;
- measured unit-cost or cycle-time improvement;
- revenue/retention/conversion or loss benefit;
- controlled quality and compliance outcomes;
- recurring monetization or durable internal margin;
- proprietary data/workflow advantage;
- limited incremental capital and remediation burden;
- per-share materiality.

- productivity claims lack denominator or baseline;

- AI revenue is reclassified existing software rather than incremental.

## 15. Structural rerating archetypes

Required display:

- law/rule/exemption type;
- final/proposed/temporary state;
- effective date;
- eligible actors/assets;
- remaining dependencies;
- current revenue materiality.

### 15.2 Adoption rerating

Usage or client count crosses from experimental to commercially relevant.

Required:

- active users/institutions;
- volume/value;
- repeat usage;
- revenue/take rate;
- cost and loss;
- retention.

### 15.3 Cost-structure rerating

Automation/core modernization lowers expense or releases capacity.

Required:

- baseline unit cost/headcount/cycle time;
- implementation and parallel-run cost;
- realized savings;
- reinvestment;
- error/remediation;
- per-share margin/FCF.

### 15.4 Business-mix rerating

A firm shifts toward recurring software/data/fees or capital-light distribution.

Required:

- old/new revenue mix;
- margin and capital;
- organic/acquired contribution;
- cannibalization;
- durability.

### 15.5 Infrastructure-toll rerating

A utility or network gains new workflow volume.

Required:

- exact role and legal necessity;
- activity growth;
- take rate;
- alternatives/interoperability;
- pricing/capital/resilience cost.

### 15.6 Option-value rerating

Price capitalizes a plausible future product before material earnings.

Required:

- probability/scenario range;
- investment cost;
- commercialization clock;
- dilution/capital;
- valuation contribution;
- falsifiers.

## 19. Basket-admission law

A disruption slice becomes a price basket only after:

1. a precise workflow and economic mechanism;
2. legal/operational/adoption/monetization stage;
3. direct/enabler/proxy/disrupted role;
4. measured exposure and denominator;
5. source-backed company/business identity;
6. expected revenue/margin/capital bridge;
7. point-in-time membership treatment;
8. transparent weighting and benchmark;
9. overlap/double-count controls;
10. rights/evidence receipt;
11. valuation and expectation separation;
12. explicit non-trade authority.

Do not admit companies solely for announcements, pilots, patents, generic AI use or blockchain participation.
