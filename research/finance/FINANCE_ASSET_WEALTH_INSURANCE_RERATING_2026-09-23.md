## 1. Executive ruling

### R-FIN-AWI-1 — “Asset management” is not one economic model

AUM can represent materially different economics:

- high-fee active equity;
- low-fee institutional index;
- ETFs with securities-lending or ecosystem economics;
- target-date and multi-asset solutions;
- private-market commitments not yet fee-paying;
- cash management;
- retirement recordkeeping assets merely administered;
- wealth-platform assets charged through advisory, transaction, cash, lending or service fees.

AUM growth is not revenue growth until the product, fee base, average-period balance, fee rate, ownership, elimination and recognition basis are known.

### R-FIN-AWI-2 — Organic flows and market beta are separate clocks

The primary asset bridge is:

```text
beginning AUM
+ client gross inflows
− client gross outflows
− realizations / distributions not reinvested
+ market return
+ FX
+ acquisitions / dispositions / transfers
± classification changes
= ending AUM
```

A rising market can hide persistent client redemptions. A falling market can hide strong organic gathering. Both may raise or lower fees differently because the average balance and product mix matter.

### R-FIN-AWI-3 — Fee-paying, fee-generating, advised and administered assets remain distinct

- AUM may or may not pay a management fee.
- AUA may generate recordkeeping or administrative revenue without investment-management economics.
- Fee-paying AUM may differ from total AUM.
- Non-fee-paying commitments or shadow AUM are potential future fee bases, not current revenue.
- Advisory assets may generate recurring fees while brokerage assets generate cash, transaction, service or lending economics.
- Index-linked assets may generate licensing revenue for one provider and management fees for another.

The product must show the exact asset population and fee mechanism.

### R-FIN-AWI-4 — Wealth platforms are distribution-and-cash systems, not only AUM businesses

A wealth platform’s earnings can depend on:

```text
client assets and organic gathering
+ advisor recruitment / retention / productivity
+ advisory mix and fee yield
+ client cash mix and sweep economics
+ trading / lending / banking / service activity
− advisor payout and acquisition consideration
− platform, compliance and technology expense
− funding, credit and capital cost where a bank is present
= per-share earnings and cash generation
```

Client cash sorting can change earnings without changing total client assets. Acquired or recruited assets can increase scale while consuming debt, equity, transition assistance and expense.

### R-FIN-AWI-5 — Retirement and recordkeeping combine participant, asset and spread clocks

Retirement providers may earn:

- participant-based administration and recordkeeping fees;
- asset-based advisory fees;
- proprietary investment-management fees;
- stable-value or general-account spread income;
- advisory or managed-account fees;
- workplace-benefit revenue;
- insurance or annuity economics.

Participant accounts, plan assets, AUA, proprietary AUM and general-account liabilities cannot be collapsed into one asset metric.

### R-FIN-AWI-6 — P&C insurance rerates on risk-adjusted book-value compounding

The core P&C bridge is:

```text
written premium and exposure
→ earned premium over coverage period
− current-accident-year attritional loss
− catastrophe loss
± prior-year reserve development
− underwriting expense
= underwriting income

invested float
× portfolio yield / total return
− financing / holding-company cost
± tax / other
= total earnings and capital generation
```

The primary long-run question is whether the insurer compounds book or tangible book value per share at an attractive return through the cycle without hidden reserve deficiency or excessive tail risk.

### R-FIN-AWI-7 — Premium growth is not value creation without rate adequacy and exposure control

Premium can grow through:

- exposure-unit growth;
- higher insured values;
- rate increases;
- mix shift;
- new business;
- retention;
- acquisitions;
- reinstatement premium;
- FX;
- inflation.

Only the part that earns an adequate risk-adjusted return after claims, expenses, reinsurance and capital supports durable rerating.

### R-FIN-AWI-8 — Reserve quality must be visible

The product must separate:

- current accident year from prior accident years;
- reported from paid loss;
- case reserves from IBNR;
- frequency from severity;
- catastrophe from attritional loss;
- favorable from adverse prior-year development;
- gross from ceded and net loss;
- nominal from discounted reserves where applicable.

A low combined ratio supported by reserve releases is different from one supported by current underwriting margin.

### R-FIN-AWI-9 — Reinsurance is a price, capital and tail-risk cycle

Reinsurance economics depend on:

- cedent demand;
- risk-adjusted rate and terms;
- attachment points and limits;
- industry and alternative capital;
- retrocession availability;
- catastrophe and casualty loss experience;
- collateral and trapped capital;
- investment income;
- third-party capital and fee income;
- capital return.

A hard market is valuable only while rates and terms exceed expected loss, expenses and cost of capital. Premium growth after terms weaken may be a de-rating signal.

### R-FIN-AWI-10 — Life and annuity businesses require separate spread, insurance and optionality clocks

The economic bridge can include:

```text
premiums / deposits / account values
→ spread income or fee income
+ mortality / morbidity / longevity margin
− acquisition and servicing expense
± lapse and policyholder behavior
± market-risk-benefit and hedge result
± annual assumption review / reserve refinement
− statutory capital and liquidity cost
= distributable earnings and book-value change
```

GAAP net income, adjusted operating income, statutory income, cash remittance and economic value may diverge materially.

### R-FIN-AWI-11 — Brokers, MGAs and data providers are not underwriting carriers

An insurance broker generally earns commissions and fees without retaining the carrier’s insured loss. Its drivers include client exposures, insurance rates, retention, new business, placement volume, consulting demand, acquisitions, fiduciary interest, compensation and integration.

A data/claims provider may earn recurring subscription and transaction revenue from embedded models, forms, rules, fraud, repair or analytics. It is exposed to insurance industry activity and budgets but does not automatically bear underwriting risk.

### R-FIN-AWI-12 — The combined `insurance` basket is analytically insufficient

At minimum the product must distinguish:

- personal P&C carriers;
- commercial P&C and specialty carriers;
- reinsurers;
- life and annuity writers;
- insurance brokers;
- MGAs and delegated underwriting platforms;
- claims, data and analytics providers;
- insurance software and workflow providers.

Price co-movement or conventional sector membership does not make these businesses economically interchangeable.

### R-FIN-AWI-13 — Existing owner boundaries remain intact

This program consumes accepted company, earnings, rates, market, sector, valuation, revisions, evidence and identity owners. It creates no second:

- AUM or fund-flow truth store;
- security master;
- insurance reserve ledger;
- valuation engine;
- consensus history;
- price-leadership state machine;
- canonical theme graph;
- publication plane.

### R-FIN-AWI-14 — Research context has no direct trade authority

The framework may describe rerating mechanisms, conflicts, historical states and watch conditions. It may not rank, gate, size or execute trades, or alter Prophet/portfolio authority.

## 2. Asset-management economic architecture

### 2.1 The eight clocks

1. **Gross-sales clock** — demand by channel, vehicle, geography and product.
2. **Redemption clock** — loss of assets, mandate terminations, retirement withdrawals and realizations.
3. **Market/FX clock** — beta and currency movement in reported AUM.
4. **Performance clock** — alpha, benchmark rank, capacity and Morningstar/consultant/platform placement.
5. **Fee-mix clock** — active/passive, retail/institutional, public/private, equity/fixed income, base/performance fees.
6. **Expense and operating-leverage clock** — compensation, distribution, data, technology and fixed costs.
7. **Capital-allocation clock** — dividends, buybacks, seed capital, acquisitions, earnouts and debt.
8. **Expectation/valuation/price clock** — consensus, multiple, positioning and recognition.

### 2.2 Revenue bridge

```text
average fee-paying AUM
× effective base-fee rate
+ performance / incentive fee
+ securities lending / administration / technology / other
+ carried-interest or capital-allocation income where applicable
− distribution and servicing pass-through
− compensation and performance-linked expense
− technology / occupancy / G&A
= operating income and free cash flow
```

### 2.3 Required AUM decomposition

Every reported AUM change should preserve the applicable dimensions:

```text
client type: retail / institutional / intermediary / retirement
style: active / index / ETF / cash / multi-asset / alternatives
asset class: equity / fixed income / private markets / cash / real assets
vehicle: mutual fund / ETF / CIT / SMA / separate account / private fund / model / CLO
fee state: fee-paying / non-fee-paying / unfunded / advised / administered
flow state: gross sale / redemption / net flow / realization / reinvested distribution
change source: market / FX / acquisition / disposition / transfer / reclassification
```

### 2.4 Rerating conditions

A traditional asset manager can rerate when:

- organic net flows inflect from persistent outflow to stabilization or inflow;
- investment performance improves in commercially important strategies;
- flow mix shifts toward higher-fee or strategically durable products;
- fee compression slows;
- new vehicles, channels or geographies expand distribution;
- private-market or solution commitments convert into fee-paying assets;
- expense growth remains below recurring revenue growth;
- excess cash is returned without underinvesting in the franchise;
- acquisitions prove per-share accretive after earnouts and amortization.

### 2.5 De-rating conditions

- market-driven AUM growth conceals organic outflows;
- inflows concentrate in low-fee products while higher-fee franchises shrink;
- investment underperformance damages retention and platform placement;
- distribution expense or compensation absorbs fee growth;
- capacity constraints limit sales in successful strategies;
- acquisition scale obscures organic deterioration;
- performance fees or capital-allocation income are annualized as recurring;
- share repurchases merely offset compensation dilution;
- a high market multiple discounts durable flows that fail to arrive.

### 2.6 Valuation anchors

Primary:

- normalized P/E and FCF yield;
- recurring base-fee earnings and margin;
- organic flow rate and effective fee rate;
- net cash or debt and capital-return capacity.

Secondary:

- EV/EBITDA;
- sum-of-parts for public-market management, private markets, technology, data and balance-sheet investments;
- price-to-AUM only as a descriptive cross-check with fee-rate and margin controls, never a direct valuation rule.

## 3. ETF, index and solution economics

### 3.1 ETF growth is not a uniform fee opportunity

ETF economics vary by:

- sponsor fee rate;
- average AUM;
- securities lending;
- index licensing;
- market-making and ecosystem relationships;
- product age and seed capital;
- distribution and waiver;
- active versus index construction;
- asset class;
- product concentration.

A high-flow flagship ETF may produce less incremental fee revenue per dollar than a smaller active or specialty vehicle.

### 3.2 Index-provider economics remain separate

The index owner may earn asset-linked royalties and data/license fees. The asset manager may earn management fees. The exchange and market maker may earn trading economics. One pool of ETF AUM can support several separate companies without being counted as the same revenue exposure.

### 3.3 Solution and target-date economics

Target-date, multi-asset, fiduciary and outsourcing mandates can improve retention and expand wallet share, but the product must preserve:

- underlying proprietary versus third-party asset mix;
- participant/plan/client count;
- AUM and AUA;
- investment-management fee;
- administration fee;
- acquired versus organic mandate;
- implementation/funding lag;
- target-date contributions and retirement withdrawals.

## 4. Wealth-platform and adviser economics

### 4.1 The eight clocks

1. **Advisor clock** — headcount, recruiting, retention, productivity and succession.
2. **Asset-gathering clock** — organic net new assets and client acquisition.
3. **Market clock** — appreciation/depreciation of client assets.
4. **Advisory-mix clock** — fee-based assets, pricing, managed solutions and advisor payout.
5. **Cash clock** — client cash allocation, sweep balances, money funds, deposit yield and funding use.
6. **Engagement clock** — trades, margin loans, securities lending, banking, trust and service activity.
7. **Expense/integration clock** — transition assistance, acquisition, platform, technology, compliance and compensation.
8. **Expectation/valuation/price clock**.

### 4.2 Wealth earnings bridge

```text
average advisory assets × net advisory yield
+ brokerage / transaction revenue
+ cash and sweep economics
+ bank net interest income / lending income where present
+ service / platform / trust / asset-management fees
− advisor payout and recruiting assistance
− interest / funding / credit losses
− platform / technology / compliance / integration expense
= pre-tax earnings and free cash flow
```

### 4.3 Organic versus acquired or recruited assets

Reported net new assets require separate fields for:

- same-store organic client flows;
- recruited advisors and their transferred assets;
- acquired institutions or advisor businesses;
- onboarding and conversion timing;
- offboarding and planned separations;
- market change;
- internal channel transfers.

Recruiting can be economically attractive, but it is not free organic growth.

### 4.4 Cash sorting

Client movement from bank deposits or sweep programs into money-market funds, purchased securities or advisory solutions can:

- reduce spread revenue;
- alter funding mix;
- increase asset-management or service fees;
- change liquidity and duration;
- change the apparent relationship between total client assets and earnings.

The product must show cash population, yield, third-party versus affiliated location, and revenue mechanism.

### 4.5 Wealth rerating conditions

- sustained organic gathering and account growth;
- higher productive advisor count without deteriorating economics;
- increased fee-based/advisory penetration at stable net yield;
- recovery in cash earnings without coercive client economics;
- improving operating leverage after platform investment;
- acquisition or recruiting cohorts meet retention and payback expectations;
- per-share FCF grows after dilution, debt and transition assistance;
- broader client engagement improves revenue durability.

### 4.6 Wealth de-rating conditions

- market appreciation hides weak organic flows;
- headline net new assets are acquisition or recruited assets with high cost;
- cash sorting weakens revenue more than advisory mix offsets;
- advisor payout and platform cost rise faster than gross profit;
- client concentration or advisor attrition increases;
- bank balance-sheet risk grows faster than wealth economics;
- acquisition-driven share issuance or debt dilutes per-share returns.

### 4.7 Valuation anchors

- normalized P/E and FCF for platform/distribution businesses;
- price-to-tangible-book and ROTCE where banking is material;
- sum-of-parts for wealth, bank, capital markets and asset management;
- client assets and advisor count only as operating denominators, not stand-alone valuation measures.

## 6. P&C insurance economics

### 6.1 The ten clocks

1. **Exposure clock** — policies, vehicles, payroll, property value, limits and insured units.
2. **Rate clock** — written-rate change by line, class, geography and layer.
3. **Earned-premium clock** — lag from binding/writing to earned revenue.
4. **Frequency clock** — claim count per exposure.
5. **Severity clock** — cost per claim and social/economic inflation.
6. **Catastrophe clock** — event losses, reinstatements and annual aggregate risk.
7. **Reserve clock** — current estimate, IBNR, paid loss and prior-year development.
8. **Reinsurance clock** — ceded premium, recoverables, attachment, limits and counterparty.
9. **Investment/capital clock** — float, yield, duration, unrealized change, statutory/RBC capital and return.
10. **Expectation/valuation/price clock**.

### 6.2 Underwriting bridge

```text
net premiums written
± unearned-premium change
= net premiums earned

net premiums earned
− net losses and loss-adjustment expense
− underwriting expense
= underwriting income
```

### 6.3 Combined-ratio anatomy

```text
loss and LAE ratio
+ expense ratio
= combined ratio
```

Required visible decomposition:

```text
current accident year ex-cat
+ catastrophe effect
+ prior-year development effect
= reported loss/combined ratio
```

A combined ratio below 100 indicates underwriting profit under the issuer’s accounting basis, not total economic return or reserve certainty.

### 6.4 Rate adequacy

The product should compare, where evidence permits:

```text
written rate change
versus
expected prospective loss-cost trend
```

with explicit limits:

- exposure change and mix can distort average rate;
- reported premium includes inflation and new business;
- rate earned with lag;
- casualty-tail emergence can take years;
- policy terms, attachment points and limits matter.

### 6.5 P&C rerating conditions

- prospective rate exceeds loss-cost trend with disciplined growth;
- current-accident-year ex-cat combined ratio improves;
- reserve development remains credible across vintages;
- premium growth comes from adequate business rather than chasing volume;
- reinsurance protects tail without destroying economics;
- recurring investment income rises on a sound portfolio;
- statutory capital supports growth and accretive capital return;
- BVPS/TBVPS compounds through catastrophe and market cycles.

### 6.6 P&C de-rating conditions

- premium growth outruns rate adequacy;
- adverse reserve development emerges in casualty or other long-tail lines;
- favorable development masks weak current underwriting;
- catastrophe load or climate/geographic concentration rises without price/terms;
- reinsurance cost, attachment or credit risk worsens;
- investment risk increases to support earnings;
- buybacks above intrinsic/book economics dilute future value;
- reported ROE relies on unusually benign loss years.

### 6.7 Valuation anchors

Primary:

- P/BV or P/TBV versus normalized ROE/ROE volatility and BVPS compounding;
- normalized operating P/E.

Secondary:

- underwriting franchise SOTP by personal, commercial, specialty, reinsurance, mortgage insurance or life operations;
- capital return and excess capital;
- catastrophe-normalized and reserve-normalized earnings.

→ new policies

→ policies in force

→ written premium

## 9. Reinsurance economics

### 9.1 Profit bridge

```text
risk-adjusted premium and terms
− expected attritional / catastrophe / casualty loss
− acquisition and operating expense
− retrocession cost
= underwriting margin

+ investment income on float
+ third-party capital / fee income
± mark-to-market and reserve development
− cost of capital
= book-value and distributable-earnings change
```

### 9.2 Market-cycle indicators

- renewal pricing and rate-on-line;
- attachment points, limits and exclusions;
- cedent retentions;
- capacity and new capital;
- catastrophe-bond and ILS spreads/issuance;
- trapped collateral;
- retrocession availability;
- loss-free versus loss-affected accounts;
- premium growth by class;
- modeled versus actual loss and reserve development.

### 9.3 Reinsurance rerating conditions

- terms remain adequate after new capacity enters;
- growth is concentrated in attractive layers/classes;
- book-value compounding persists through loss events;
- third-party capital creates fee income without hidden guarantees;
- retrocession and collateral are efficient;
- repurchases are accretive and do not impair catastrophe capacity;
- reserve and model credibility remain strong.

## 10. Life, annuity and retirement-spread economics

### 10.1 The nine clocks

1. Sales/deposit and account-value growth.
2. Premium and fee recognition.
3. General-account asset yield and reinvestment.
4. Crediting rate and policyholder spread.
5. Mortality, morbidity and longevity experience.
6. Lapse, surrender and policyholder behavior.
7. Hedging and market-risk-benefit valuation.
8. Statutory/RBC capital, liquidity and remittance.
9. Expectations/valuation/price.

### 10.2 Earnings bridge

```text
fee income
+ investment spread
+ insurance margin
+ asset-management / retirement income
− benefits and claims
− credited interest
− acquisition / servicing / distribution expense
− hedge cost and capital cost
± assumption / reserve / market updates
= operating and statutory earnings
```

### 10.3 Critical distinctions

- sales are not earnings;
- deposits are not revenue;
- premium can be offset by a corresponding policyholder liability/benefit;
- adjusted operating income is issuer-defined and requires reconciliation;
- GAAP market-risk-benefit movement can differ from hedge economics;
- AOCI affects reported book but not always management’s adjusted book measure;
- statutory capital and cash remittance govern shareholder distributions;
- pension-risk-transfer premium can create scale while adding longevity, spread and capital exposure.

### 10.4 Rerating conditions

- business mix shifts toward understandable, capital-efficient and recurring earnings;
- spread income improves with matched assets/liabilities;
- mortality/morbidity/lapse experience remains favorable or well priced;
- assumption reviews become less volatile and more credible;
- legacy/runoff exposure declines;
- statutory capital, cash generation and remittance improve;
- hedging works across stressed markets;
- adjusted book value and per-share operating earnings compound.

### 10.5 De-rating conditions

- reserve or assumption charges reveal weaker economics;
- new business is volume-rich but capital- or guarantee-heavy;
- spread income depends on greater credit, duration or liquidity risk;
- policyholder behavior differs from assumptions;
- hedges, MRBs and GAAP results become opaque;
- statutory capital traps cash;
- book-value improvement is driven only by rates/AOCI rather than operating compounding.

### 10.6 Valuation anchors

- P/BV or adjusted book value excluding specified AOCI/MRB effects, with transparent definition;
- adjusted operating P/E;
- free cash flow/cash remittance and capital return;
- SOTP by protection, retirement, asset management, international and runoff businesses.

## 11. Insurance brokers and advisory firms

### 11.1 Revenue bridge

```text
client exposure / payroll / insured value / benefit lives
× insurance premium/rate environment
× commission or fee arrangement
+ net new business
+ retention
+ consulting / analytics / placement / reinsurance fees
+ fiduciary interest
+ acquisitions
± FX / dispositions
= reported revenue
```

Then:

```text
reported revenue
− producer and employee compensation
− integration / technology / occupancy / support
− interest on acquisition debt
= operating income and FCF
```

### 11.2 Organic-growth law

Organic or underlying revenue should separate:

- existing-client exposure and pricing;
- retention;
- net new business;
- acquisition revenue;
- disposition;
- FX;
- fiduciary interest;
- one-time project/advisory work.

A hard insurance market can support commission growth, but brokers can still grow through exposure, new business, consulting and acquisitions as premium rates moderate.

### 11.3 Rerating conditions

- organic revenue remains durable across rate cycles;
- retention and net new business remain strong;
- margins expand after appropriate producer investment;
- acquisitions meet retention, synergy and debt/payback goals;
- FCF conversion supports deleveraging and capital return;
- data/analytics and advisory deepen client relationships;
- fiduciary-interest normalization is understood rather than mistaken for core deterioration.

### 11.4 De-rating conditions

- acquisition contribution is presented as organic quality;
- leverage, equity issuance or earnouts overwhelm per-share accretion;
- integration and compensation costs persist;
- rate moderation exposes weak client/volume growth;
- producer attrition harms retention;
- fiduciary interest or restructuring is annualized as core earnings;
- headline margin expands through underinvestment or temporary compensation timing.

### 11.5 Valuation anchors

- normalized P/E and FCF yield;
- EV/EBITDA with acquisition/debt controls;
- organic growth, margin and FCF conversion;
- acquisition-adjusted per-share growth and leverage.

## 12. Claims, data, analytics and insurance technology

### 12.1 Business archetypes

- policy forms, rules and loss costs;
- catastrophe and risk models;
- claims estimation and repair workflow;
- fraud and identity databases;
- underwriting data and decision tools;
- broker/carrier core workflow software;
- distribution and quote platforms;
- telematics and IoT data;
- regulatory and actuarial services.

### 12.2 Earnings bridge

```text
subscription ACV / installed customers / covered modules
+ price / cross-sell / new products
+ transaction volume and usage
+ consulting / implementation
− data acquisition / cloud / support / R&D / sales expense
= recurring revenue, margin and FCF
```

### 12.3 Rerating conditions

- subscription mix and retention remain high;
- price reflects product value without churn;
- cross-sell and workflow penetration deepen;
- transaction/claims exposure adds growth without excessive cyclicality;
- model and data quality remain credible;
- organic growth exceeds expense growth;
- FCF conversion and capital return improve.

### 12.4 De-rating conditions

- acquisition or price obscures weak usage/retention;
- insurers internalize or commoditize functionality;
- AI lowers switching cost or substitutes for proprietary workflows;
- model error, cyber, regulatory or data-rights events damage trust;
- consulting growth is mistaken for recurring subscription growth;
- capitalized development or stock compensation weakens owner earnings.

### 12.5 Valuation anchors

- recurring revenue growth, retention, organic margin and FCF;
- P/E, EV/EBITDA and FCF yield;
- revenue multiple only with mature-margin and cash-conversion bridge.

## 13. Cross-subtheme rerating archetypes

### 13.1 Flow inflection

Examples:

- active-manager redemptions stabilize;
- ETF or retirement flows accelerate;
- wealth organic assets improve;
- broker new business/retention broadens.

Required confirmation:

- source-native flow population;
- organic versus acquired/recruited;
- fee-rate implication;
- durability and distribution breadth;
- expectation state.

### 13.2 Fee-mix upgrade

- shift toward higher-fee active/private/solution assets;
- increased advisory share in wealth;
- proprietary or managed-account penetration in retirement;
- subscription mix in data/software.

Risk: higher fee may carry higher compensation, distribution, performance or capital cost.

### 13.3 Underwriting-quality rerating

- current-year loss ratio improves;
- reserve confidence increases;
- pricing remains ahead of loss cost;
- catastrophe exposure and reinsurance become more efficient;
- BVPS compounds through adverse events.

### 13.4 Capital-cycle rerating

- insurer/reinsurer capital scarcity supports price/terms;
- life insurer statutory cash improves;
- excess capital enables growth and accretive buybacks;
- acquisition leverage declines at brokers/wealth platforms.

### 13.5 Business-mix transformation

- manager adds technology/data/private markets;
- wealth shifts toward recurring advisory economics;
- insurer exits capital-intensive legacy products;
- broker builds advisory/data capability;
- retirement provider improves fee/spread balance.

### 13.6 Risk-discount normalization

- reserve, catastrophe, assumption, liquidity, integration or regulatory uncertainty declines;
- transparency improves;
- required return falls before current earnings fully recover.

### 13.7 Overearning and de-rating

- benign catastrophe year;
- unusually favorable reserve development;
- high floating rates or fiduciary interest;
- temporary market appreciation/performance fees;
- unusually hard insurance pricing;
- cash-sorting benefit;
- acquisition accounting or restructuring.

The product must identify when current earnings are above a credible normalized path.

## 14. Macro and market transmission matrix

| Driver | Asset manager | Wealth/retirement | P&C/reinsurance | Life/annuity | Brokers/data |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Equity-market rise | raises AUM/fees; may mask outflows | raises client assets/advisory fees | raises investment assets/equity; mixed exposure | improves fee bases, may move MRBs/hedges | raises wealth/investment-related fees |
| Equity-market fall | lowers fees and performance economics | lowers client assets; may raise trading | investment marks/capital effects | guarantee/hedge and account-value effects | project/market-linked revenue may soften |
| Higher short rates | cash-management yield; mixed flows | cash/sweep income but sorting/funding shifts | higher new-money yield | higher spread potential, surrender/competition risk | fiduciary interest tailwind |
| Falling short rates | cash fee/yield pressure | sweep/spread pressure; possible risk-asset migration | reinvestment yield pressure | spread compression; lower surrender incentives possible | fiduciary-interest headwind |
| Higher long yields | bond AUM marks; allocation shifts | fixed-income demand; mortgage/lending effects | reinvestment yield improves, marks/capital move | spread potential and liability valuation; duration risk | mixed advisory/insurance activity |
| Inflation | nominal asset/premium/exposure growth; expense pressure | asset/payroll growth and expense | severity/social inflation, rate response | expense and claim assumptions | insured values/payroll help revenue, wage cost rises |
| Economic growth | retirement contributions, assets and risk demand | client gathering/engagement | exposure growth and better credit; claims mix | sales and employment-linked protection | new business, exposure and consulting demand |
| Recession | outflows, market beta | lower assets, cash behavior, credit | lower exposure; frequency/severity/credit can vary | lapse/mortality/credit and sales effects | retention resilient but new business/M&A softer |
| Catastrophe | limited direct | limited direct | claims, reinsurance price/capital cycle | usually limited, event-specific mortality | broker/reinsurance/data demand can rise |
| Credit-spread widening | fixed-income marks/flows; private-market marks | client allocation and bank exposure | portfolio marks and credit risk | spread assets, capital, defaults | client risk demand; acquisition funding cost |
| Regulation | fees/products/distribution | fiduciary/retirement/cash rules | rate, capital and product rules | capital, product, accounting and conduct | advisory demand but compliance cost |

No matrix cell is a deterministic stock call. Business mix, hedging, capital and expectation state control the sign and magnitude.

## 16. Required product conflict states

The Finance dossier should show plain-language conflicts such as:

- **AUM up; organic flows negative.** Market gains or acquisitions are carrying the asset base.
- **Flows positive; fee yield falling.** Growth is concentrated in lower-fee products.
- **Client assets up; cash earnings down.** Cash sorting or rate changes offset advisory growth.
- **Advisor count up; per-share economics down.** Recruiting or acquisition cost is outrunning revenue.
- **Premium growth strong; underwriting weaker.** Rate/terms are not covering loss and expense.
- **Combined ratio improves; reserve quality weakens.** Favorable prior-year development or lower reserve coverage needs review.
- **Underwriting weak; book value grows.** Investment gains or capital actions are offsetting insurance losses.
- **Life operating earnings up; statutory cash constrained.** Shareholder remittance remains limited.
- **Broker revenue up; organic growth modest.** Acquisition contribution dominates.
- **Data revenue recurring; price already discounts high durability.** Valuation risk remains separate.
- **Fundamentals improve; stock diverges.** Expectations, multiple or positioning may have turned first.
- **Stock rerates; estimate path does not confirm.** Narrative or risk-discount change is leading the evidence.

## 22. Primary-source observations anchoring this tranche

These examples establish analytical distinctions; they do not imply investment attractiveness.

- BlackRock reported $14.0 trillion of AUM at year-end 2025, $698.3 billion of total net inflows and $527 billion of ETF net inflows. Its institutional index assets represented a much larger share of long-term AUM than of long-term base fees, demonstrating why AUM mix matters more than asset scale alone. `[AWI-01]`
- T. Rowe Price reported more than 90% of net revenue tied to investment-advisory fees, 2025 net revenue of about $7.3 billion, and an asset base shaped by market movement, net cash flows and declining fee mix. This illustrates a traditional active-manager rerating problem rather than an ETF-scale problem. `[AWI-02]`
- Franklin Resources ended fiscal 2025 with $1.66 trillion of AUM but substantial long-term net outflows, with market change and cash-management flows offsetting part of the organic pressure. `[AWI-03]`
- Schwab reported $11.90 trillion of client assets and $519.4 billion of 2025 core net new assets, while its earnings also depended on client cash, funding, lending and engagement. Client assets therefore do not map one-for-one into advisory revenue. `[AWI-04]`
- LPL reported $2.37 trillion of total advisory and brokerage assets, but total net new assets of $431.5 billion included acquisition/recruiting effects while organic net new assets were $146.5 billion. `[AWI-05]`
- Progressive reported $83.2 billion of 2025 net premiums written, 38.6 million policies in force and a 12.6% underwriting margin. The company’s stated profitability discipline demonstrates that premium growth and underwriting quality are jointly necessary. `[AWI-06]`
- Arch reported 22.6% growth in book value per share during 2025 and explicitly identifies book-value-per-share growth as a key measure of value creation. `[AWI-07]`
- RenaissanceRe reported 2025 underwriting income, investment income and fee income as separate profit drivers, while large-loss events affected the combined ratio and book-value path. `[AWI-08]`
- MetLife’s 2025 results included premiums, policy fees, investment income and issuer-defined adjusted earnings, while its retirement business disclosed that premium changes can be offset by corresponding policyholder benefits. `[AWI-09]`
- Marsh, Aon and Gallagher disclosures separately identify organic/underlying growth, acquisition contribution, margins and integration. Reported revenue growth alone cannot establish franchise quality or per-share accretion. `[AWI-10]`
- Verisk reported 83% of 2025 revenue from hosted subscriptions and separately described underwriting and claims growth, supporting a recurring data/workflow valuation model rather than an underwriting-carrier model. `[AWI-11]`
