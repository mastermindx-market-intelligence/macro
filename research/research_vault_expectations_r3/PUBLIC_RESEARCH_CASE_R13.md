# Institutional intelligence worked case: price pressure is not the whole tariff story

**Human-authored research/design example, not a generated Brain answer, a MarketDesk download, a production record, a forecast, or investment advice.** Public sources were read through web text and PDF screenshots; the original PDF was NOT obtained in the working filesystem. No PDF fingerprint or production-source admission is claimed.

## The user question

“Do these studies imply only higher prices, or do we also need to investigate sales volumes and product mix?”

## Source-grounded answer

The April 8, 2026 Federal Reserve staff note by Minton, Ray and Somale estimates effects of tariffs implemented through November 2025 on prices through February 2026. It reports a 3.1% increase in core-goods PCE prices and a 0.8% increase in the broader core-PCE price level. Those concern different aggregates, not additive components. Its pass-through benchmark is dollar-for-dollar transmission of tariff-related acquisition costs, with effects building over months. This is not a statement that September inflation is currently 0.8 percentage points higher. [F1]

The captured Hacıoğlu-Hoke/Feler working paper uses household transactions and a sentiment survey. Its reported 15% and 20% price coefficients use different exposure definitions; they must not be summed. The authors also analyze spending contraction and shifts toward essentials and cheaper varieties. The result is author research, not a Federal Reserve policy decision. Its observed title page is dated May 21, 2026. [F2]

**My interpretation for the product:** these sources motivate separate questions about pricing, quantities, product mix, and margins. They do not supply a company-specific earnings revision or an investment signal. A business may pass through part of an input cost yet still experience lower units or a less profitable sales mix. The company-specific link requires that issuer's own evidence; it cannot be manufactured from the macro label.

## Why “20%” and “100%” are not automatically contradictory

The comparison needs the quantity being measured, its denominator, target population, observation period, and model. Calling both numbers “pass-through” does not align those properties. The examined staff note explicitly uses a dollar-for-dollar cost benchmark; the working paper distinguishes tariff-rate exposure from import-penetration-adjusted exposure. [F1–F2]

A deliberately **fictional arithmetic illustration** makes the denominator issue concrete. An item sells for $100 and its tariff-exposed acquisition cost is $20. An additional 10% tariff adds $2 to that cost. Passing the entire $2 through raises the retail price to $102: 100% of the added dollars, but a 2% retail-price change per 10% tariff change, a ratio of 0.20. These are two descriptions of the same invented arithmetic. This does not estimate either paper's actual cost shares or establish that all empirical differences between them are resolved.

The machine should therefore produce `COMPARABILITY_NOT_ESTABLISHED`, not average the coefficients or pronounce one study disproved. A deeper comparison would align samples, time windows, weighting, tariff definitions and mechanisms before calculating any disagreement.

## A real version qualification issue

The paper's landing page labels it revised in August 2026 and uses DOI suffix `2026.035r1`. The linked PDF observed through the web tool has a May 21 title page and a cover citation ending `2026.035`. [F2–F3]

Both observations should be retained. This is an unresolved metadata/version-label discrepancy, not proof of a stale web server or a complete comparison of the two revisions. A document fingerprint alone cannot prove that a landing page's revision metadata describes those bytes. In a customer answer, identify the captured version honestly rather than silently declaring it the latest August revision.

## What this would contribute to Mastermind

For Mastermind AI, the output is a concise answer with a distinction between the studies' measures. For a company brief, it supplies questions to check against issuer evidence: changes in realized pricing, units, mix, input costs and margin assumptions. For a private portfolio brief, it can explain why a shared business assumption deserves review only after existing company relationships support the link. For news, it can distinguish a new estimate, a new observation window, a changed method and a genuinely comparable revision.

For original editorial, the useful contribution is the explanation of different denominators and business channels—not a reposted report. Publication and source-use decisions still apply. No social post, article, customer brief or portfolio alert was published by this exercise.

The long-term research asset should retain the **definitions behind a claim**, not merely its number. A source-bound graph that loses these definitions could preserve provenance while manufacturing false agreement or false disagreement.
