## 1. User question and useful answer

**When a food theme grows, which part of the chain actually retains more profit and cash?**

The answer requires the commercial role, not just the theme. An ingredient/formulation business sells functionality and material to another business; a retailer sells an assortment and shopping service to the final customer. Changes in ingredient invoices, shopper payments, conversion contribution, store operating costs and invested cash are not interchangeable observations.

This case compares Ingredion and Sprouts as contrasting roles, NOT as a demonstrated supplier-customer pair. It does not assert their sales describe the same products or consumers. ## 4. A non-additive adverse scenario

The following is an ORIGINAL SYNTHETIC EXAMPLE, not an estimated Ingredion/Sprouts contract. Assume one physical unit, no inventory movement, and ignore all costs beyond the stated intermediate purchase.

| Payment/limited contribution | Initial | Input-cost pass-through scenario |
|---|---:|---:|
| Input purchase |10|8|
| Producer invoice to retailer |16|14|
| Final shopper payment |25|23|
| Producer conversion contribution before other costs |6|6|
| Retail gross contribution before other costs |9|9|

The shopper pays less while producer sales decline and both limited contribution dollars remain unchanged. The retail contribution percentage rises because its denominator falls. Initial transaction revenues sum to51, but final spending is25; the intermediate purchase is not extra end demand. These identities do not estimate value added for any actual issuer or national economy.

Now add an adverse possibility: preserving those gross contributions requires more deliveries, slower collections, marketing or new capacity. Either business can retain less cash despite an apparently favorable gross result. Conversely, current cash can fall because productive investments precede their benefits. Without incremental cohort cash and the investment counterfactual, neither verdict is established.

This is why a useful food-chain explanation needs both price transmission and the full cost of serving the customer. A fixed conversion fee, cost-plus contract, percentage markup and spot purchase can react differently. The actual commercial formula remains unknown where undisclosed; this case does not assign one from the company's sector label.

## 5. Competing interpretations and next observations

**Ingredient hypothesis:** differentiated formulation/texture activity generates repeat business with adequate contribution after qualification, production, customer support and capital. The strongest alternative is favorable mix or local recovery offset by lower utilization, weak other businesses or expensive growth. The discriminator is matched product/customer activity, retained conversion economics, working capital and investment, not a group sales increase or a broad protein label.

**Retail hypothesis:** the customer proposition supports productive expansion and durable mature-store economics. The strongest alternative is footprint growth masking weaker existing customers or costly capacity. The discriminator is a disclosed comparable-store/customer population plus separate opening cohorts, with cash investment and cannibalization. A consolidated profit bridge cannot settle new-store payback.

**Chain hypothesis:** lower intermediate prices create value for the end customer without destroying the supplier's retained contribution. The alternative is concessions needed to hold volume, or savings absorbed elsewhere. A valid test follows the same SKU, contract revision, downstream price and paid units across a documented commercial relationship. Here that matched chain is NOT established.
