# Case 1 — warehouse automation: Symbotic / Walmart

## Buyer job and deployment object

**REPORTED FACT — Symbotic Form 10-Q, period ended 2026-06-27.** Symbotic states that its systems automate depalletizing, storage, selection and palletization of pallets/cases/eaches in warehouses. It reports systems operational at major retailers including Walmart and describes its 2025 Walmart agreement for development, manufacture and installation of automated online-pickup/delivery systems.

Source: SEC Form 10-Q filed 2026-08-05:  
https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1837240/000183724026000043/sym-20260627.htm

**REPORTED FACT — Walmart FY26 Q4 earnings presentation.** Walmart reported that about 50% of eCommerce fulfillment-center volume was automated, about 60% of stores were receiving some level of automated freight, and 23 of 42 regional distribution centers were in various stages of automation retrofit.

Source: Walmart FY26 Q4 earnings presentation, filed 2026-02-19:  
https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/104169/000010416926000032/earningspresentationfy26.htm

**LIMIT.** Walmart's portfolio percentages do not identify which automated volume or site is specifically attributable to Symbotic. They are buyer-side evidence that automation is productive and material in the network, not a supplier-attribution denominator.

## Contract and conversion to supplier revenue

**REPORTED FACT — Symbotic 2026-06-27 10-Q.** Unsatisfied performance obligations for contracts longer than one year were $22.5 billion at 2026-06-27; Symbotic says a substantial majority relates to undelivered/partially delivered systems under the Walmart Master Automation Agreement plus Exol. The same filing says one customer represented 90.5% of quarterly revenue and 82.8% of accounts receivable at quarter end. Customer concentration therefore has to be shown alongside growth, not hidden behind a broad warehouse-automation label.

**REPORTED FACT — deployment and operational cohorts.** Symbotic reported 77 Systems in Deployment in the quarter ended 2026-06-27, versus 46 a year earlier, and 56 Operational Systems under software-maintenance/support contracts, versus 42 a year earlier. The company attributes higher systems revenue to the larger deployment cohort and higher software-maintenance/support revenue to the larger operational cohort.

Those are different populations:
- **System in Deployment** supports installation/construction revenue conversion;
- **Operational System** supports recurring software/support and potentially operations-service economics;
- neither alone proves site throughput, uptime or buyer payback.

## Supplier economics

**REPORTED FACT — Q3 FY2026 financials.** For the quarter ended 2026-06-27, Symbotic reported:
- systems revenue $670.952m; systems cost of revenue $523.607m;
- software maintenance/support revenue $12.765m; cost $3.486m;
- operation-services revenue $37.121m; cost $32.835m;
- total revenue $720.838m; total gross profit $160.910m.

Source: Symbotic Q3 FY2026 earnings release, filed 2026-08-05:  
https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1837240/000183724026000042/q3268-k_ex991.htm

**HOUSE DERIVATION — same reported inputs, not independent evidence.**
- systems gross margin ≈ 22.0%;
- software-maintenance/support gross margin ≈ 72.7%;
- operation-services gross margin ≈ 11.5%;
- total gross margin ≈ 22.3%.

These margins are company-wide revenue-category economics, not Walmart-site margins.

**REPORTED FACT — support burden is economically real.** Symbotic defines Operation Services as training, managed/on-site assistance and other support to help customers operate the system, typically billed time-and-materials or fixed fee; spare-parts revenue and corresponding costs are included. It says higher deployment volume also increased headcount and that warranty expense partially offset systems gross-profit improvement.

This prevents a false model in which installed robotics produces high-margin recurring revenue without ongoing labor, training, spares or warranty cost.

## Buyer economics

**ATTRIBUTED BUYER ASSERTION — Walmart Q4 FY26 earnings call/presentation.** Walmart management said it was pleased with the capital investments and returns from automation and reported broader labor-productivity/inventory-management benefits in the same quarter.

Sources:
- Walmart FY26 Q4 earnings call transcript, 2026-02-19:  
  https://corporate.walmart.com/content/dam/corporate/documents/newsroom/2026/02/19/walmart-releases-q4-fy26-earnings/q4-fy26-earnings-call-transcript.pdf
- Walmart earnings presentation above.

**LIMIT.** Walmart does not disclose a Symbotic-specific ROI, site capex, labor-hours saved, uptime, throughput denominator, integration cost or payback period in the inspected sources. Consolidated Walmart ROI/capex cannot be allocated to Symbotic without unsupported attribution.

## Useful conclusion

There is strong evidence of **economic deployment**, not merely a robot catalog:
- a large contracted installation program;
- supplier revenue tied to systems being deployed;
- a separately observable operational cohort;
- recurring software/support and lower-margin operations/support revenue after go-live;
- buyer-side evidence that warehouse automation is being used at scale.

The missing link is **matched site-level buyer payback**. The case therefore supports the thesis that warehouse automation becomes economically meaningful when an installed system advances into an operational cohort that generates work plus contracted support/revenue. It does **not** yet quantify Walmart's unit labor savings or Symbotic's return on one installation.

### Strongest competing explanation

Growth may primarily reflect a concentrated capital rollout and cost-to-cost revenue recognition rather than evidence that every installed site clears an attractive mature-state customer ROI. Supplier revenue can rise during installation even before a comparable operational cohort proves steady utilization. Customer concentration also makes aggregate supplier economics unusually dependent on one buyer.

### Next discriminating observation

A matched regional-distribution-center cohort with:
- go-live/acceptance date;
- case/pallet/eaches throughput versus baseline;
- available system-hours and downtime;
- direct/remote labor hours;
- maintenance/spares;
- customer installed capital and avoided operating cost;
- software/operations-support fees;
- expansion/renewal decision.

Until that exists, buyer payback stays UNMEASURED.

# Case 2 — EV charging: EVgo owned public network, with GM availability obligations as a reliability lens

This case deliberately uses a **portfolio cohort** rather than pretending one public site represents the network. EVgo discloses delivered energy, stalls, charging revenue/cost and capital funding at portfolio level; site-level power price, rent and ROI remain largely undisclosed.

## Credible paid demand

**REPORTED FACT — EVgo Q2 2026 results, 2026-08-05.**
- public-network average daily throughput per stall: 276 kWh/day in Q2 2026 versus 281 kWh/day in Q2 2025;
- network throughput: 99 GWh versus 88 GWh, +13%;
- 3,930 EVgo public-network stalls were in operation at 2026-06-30, within 5,380 total stalls including AV and eXtend;
- charging-network revenue: $61.421m, +19%.

Source:  
https://investors.evgo.com/news-releases/news-release-details/evgo-inc-reports-second-quarter-2026-results

**HOUSE DERIVATION — endpoint approximation, NOT an exposure reconciliation (C-R2).**  
3,930 × 276 × 91 = 98,705,880 kWh, or 98.70588 GWh. The arithmetic assumes that the end-quarter public stall count supplies the full quarter's eligible exposure and that 276 kWh/day uses exactly that same exposure/weighting. Neither assumption is established by numerical closeness to 99 GWh. Actual comparable stall-days, commissioning/retirement intervals and the issuer's averaging/eligibility definition have not been qualified in this repair; actual reconciliation remains UNMEASURED. The filing's 99 GWh is PUBLIC-network throughput. No public/AV/eXtend population error is alleged or corrected.

**INTERPRETATION.** Reported total public-network energy rose while the reported average daily public-stall metric declined slightly. These aggregates alone do not identify how much energy growth came from expansion, mature-site demand, ramp, availability or weighting changes. They do not establish mature-site economics.

**Synthetic counterexample, not EVgo operating data.** One stall eligible for all 91 days and another commissioned with 46 days remaining provide 137 stall-days, not the 182 obtained from two end-period stalls × 91. At an assumed constant 276 kWh per eligible stall-day, those exposures imply 37,812 versus 50,232 kWh. An endpoint product is valid only when its exposure and rate assumptions are actually established.

## Payer and revenue composition

**REPORTED FACT — EVgo 2026-06-30 10-Q.** Q2 charging-network revenue included:
- retail charging: $37.783m;
- commercial charging: $10.820m;
- OEM charging: $3.386m;
- regulatory-credit sales: $4.690m;
- network OEM: $4.742m;
for total charging-network revenue of $61.421m.

Source: SEC Form 10-Q filed 2026-08-05:  
https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1821159/000182115926000019/evgo-20260630.htm

**HOUSE DERIVATION — portfolio intensity, not tariff.**
- direct retail + commercial + OEM charging revenue / 99 GWh ≈ $0.525 per delivered kWh;
- total charging-network revenue / 99 GWh ≈ $0.620/kWh.

The second figure is **not a charging price** because it includes regulatory credits and network/OEM revenue. Neither ratio is a site margin because power, rent and utilization vary by site. These retained aggregate quotients are not separately observed customer tariffs or a validated price input for another cohort; a ratio derived from revenue cannot independently validate that revenue. Contract, eligible energy, recognition period, discounts and other adjustments must match before using a realized yield in the measured-revenue identity below.

## Operating cost and retained charging economics

**REPORTED FACT — EVgo 10-Q.** Q2 charging-network cost of sales was $39.247m. EVgo attributes the year-over-year increase to both:
- higher energy costs from throughput; and
- higher non-energy costs, primarily maintenance activity plus rent and related expenses as the network grew.

Depreciation net of capital-build amortization was $18.842m in Q2 and increased because of network growth.

**REPORTED FACT — company charging-network gross margin.** EVgo reported Q2 charging-network gross profit of $22.174m and charging-network gross margin of 36.1%.

**HOUSE DERIVATION — same inputs.** Charging-network cost intensity ≈ $0.396 per reported kWh and gross-profit intensity ≈ $0.224/kWh. These are portfolio accounting bridges, not site contribution margins, because revenue includes non-energy items and the cost perimeter is company-defined.

## Availability: obligation is not observation

**REPORTED FACT — GM contract terms in EVgo 10-Q.** EVgo is required to maintain at least 97% network availability across GM Flagship Stalls and 95% across the rest of the GM network, where availability is defined as the share of time a charger is operational and available on the network.

**LIMIT.** The inspected filing establishes the contractual threshold, not actual measured Q2 availability. Therefore this case must display:
- contractual availability requirement = KNOWN;
- actual availability for the matched cohort = NOT ACQUIRED.

A contractual uptime target cannot be silently converted into achieved uptime.

## Capital and funding perimeter

**REPORTED FACT — Q2 capital.**
- GAAP capital expenditures: $33.823m;
- OEM infrastructure payments: $1.352m;
- proceeds from capital-build funding: $5.170m;
- total capital offsets: $6.522m;
- capital expenditures net of those offsets: $27.301m.

EVgo defines the offset metric and warns it is non-GAAP.

**REPORTED FACT — DOE financing.** At 2026-06-30 the amended DOE facility provided up to $625m of borrowings plus up to $125m capitalized interest. Eligible-project draws initially reimburse an amount equal to 80% of eligible project costs, subject to the specified leverage mechanics; 1,594 contributed public-network DC stalls secure the borrower. Outstanding DOE balance was $226.1m and the weighted-average rate on outstanding amounts was approximately 5.63%.

**INTERPRETATION.** Charging-site economics depend not only on utilization and charging margin but on funding structure and restricted cash/debt-service obligations. Subsidy/loan support lowers sponsor cash needs but does not make site economics independent of power, rent, maintenance, uptime and debt service.

### Separate accounting-profit and cash-investment views (C-R3)

The following are explanatory, scope-matched bridges, NOT a new EVgo cash-flow calculation or admitted calculator. Define R as recognized revenue for the selected business/period, C as its accrued operating costs excluding depreciation/amortization, and D&A as its depreciation/amortization charge. Then operating profit = R − C − D&A. Do not deduct D&A again when it is already included in the starting reported expense/profit measure. Cash capex is not an additional same-period depreciation charge in this operating-profit bridge.

For a separate post-tax, post-investment, pre-financing cash view, start from operating profit, add back the included noncash D&A and any separately identified other noncash items, reconcile operating working-capital/timing changes, and subtract cash taxes and cash capex once:

`CF_pre_financing = operating_profit + included_D&A + other_noncash_adjustments − change_in_operating_NWC − cash_taxes − cash_capex`

This schematic requires the chosen costs, taxes, NWC and other adjustments to have compatible definitions; it is not automatically the issuer's reported CFO or FCF. An increase in operating NWC is a use of cash. Unknown tax, working-capital, noncash, site-allocation or investment inputs remain unknown, not zero. Do not subtract a corporate D&A or capex number from a charging-network margin without a matching perimeter.

Show cash interest, new debt, debt-principal repayment and other financing separately when moving from the pre-financing view to sponsor cash. If the starting cash measure already includes interest, do not deduct it again. Loan proceeds are financing, not charging revenue or operating profit; capitalized interest and restricted cash require their own treatment. An eligible capital cash receipt may reduce gross sponsor investment only once and only if not already included in revenue/timing, the starting cash measure or net capex. Preserve the issuer's gross-capex/offset/net-capex definitions rather than adding an offset twice.

**Synthetic bridge, not issuer observations.** R=1,000, C=600 and D&A=100 give operating profit 300. Assume only D&A is noncash, all other accrual/cash timing is captured by a 40 increase in operating NWC, cash taxes are 60, and cash capex is 200. Cash is 300+100−40−60−200=100, identical to 1,000−600−40−60−200. Subtracting capex from profit without adding back included D&A incorrectly yields zero. Raising D&A to 150 while holding those assumed cash flows fixed changes profit to 250, but cash remains 100. These fixtures do not assert missing EVgo costs, taxes or working capital.

## Negative scenario

A plausible adverse path is:
1. stall count keeps rising;
2. per-stall delivered energy stays flat or declines;
3. energy, rent and maintenance consume more operating cash, while depreciation affects accounting profit and cash capex affects the separate investment view;
4. public incentives/offsets become less favorable or debt service rises;
5. reported network growth therefore fails to translate into acceptable site returns.

Q2 already demonstrates why installed-stall growth alone is insufficient: total GWh rose while average daily throughput per public stall fell from 281 to 276 kWh/day. This coexistence does not quantify expansion's causal contribution or mature-site returns.

## Useful conclusion

A charging thesis needs separate measured and prospective views, not a product of already measured total energy and capacity metadata (C-R1):

**Measured direct charging revenue:** `R_charge = sum(E_i * p_i)`, with E_i the observed delivered energy in kWh for a matched contract/customer/period cell and p_i its compatible realized net charging yield in currency/kWh. Holding measured E_i and p_i fixed, changing unrelated stall-count or availability metadata cannot change R_charge. The identity does not imply immediate cash collection; recognition/timing and any non-energy contract adjustments must be reconciled. Keep regulatory credits and separately recognized network/OEM fees outside R_charge and identify them separately in any wider revenue bridge.

**Prospective energy:** `E_hat = sum(H_i * a_i * q_i)`, with H_i scheduled eligible stall-hours over actual commissioning/retirement intervals, a_i the compatible available/scheduled fraction, and q_i kWh per AVAILABLE stall-hour for that same scope. The units close to kWh. q_i is a conditional delivered-energy rate, not a nameplate kW rating. At heterogeneous sites, retain compatible cells rather than multiplying unmatched portfolio averages. Unknown exposure, availability or intensity prevents a supported numerical estimate; a scenario must label assumptions explicitly.

**Alternative rate basis:** when q_sched_i is kWh per ALL scheduled eligible stall-hours, use `E_hat = sum(H_i * q_sched_i)` with NO additional availability factor. Availability is already reflected in that rate. Do not apply either prospective formula to an already observed network energy total.

**Synthetic dimensional checks.** Measured 600 kWh at 0.40 plus 400 kWh at 0.65 yields 500 currency units regardless of unrelated stall metadata. For a different prospective example, 1,000 scheduled stall-hours × 0.8 availability × 20 kWh/available stall-hour yields 16,000 kWh, equal to 1,000 × 16 kWh/scheduled stall-hour. Applying 0.8 again to the latter incorrectly yields 12,800 kWh. Neither example estimates EVgo revenue or energy.

Use the separately defined profit and cash-investment views above to assess economic retention. Installed ports, contractual uptime and announcements cannot replace achieved operation, measured revenue or matched cash evidence.

### Strongest competing explanation

The slight decline in average daily throughput per stall may reflect network cohort mix and newly opened/renewed sites rather than deteriorating mature-site demand. Without vintage/cohort utilization and actual availability, one cannot infer weakening site economics from the aggregate per-stall figure.

### Exact missing input

Matched site/vintage data linking:
- actual availability;
- integrated eligible stall-hours/days and the issuer's average/eligibility definition;
- sessions or kWh/stall;
- realized retail/commercial price;
- energy/demand charges;
- host rent/revenue share;
- maintenance/repair;
- site capex and offset;
- debt/subsidy attribution;
- depreciation/retirement;
- site age.

Without that, portfolio gross margin cannot be turned into a site-return claim.

# Case 3 — specialized autonomy: Caterpillar / Luck Stone Bull Run quarry

This case is stronger than a launch announcement because it has an operating chronology, measured productive work and a subsequent expansion decision. It is still incomplete financially because buyer cost and supplier contract economics are not disclosed.

## Technical availability -> operating scope

**REPORTED FACT — Caterpillar, 2026-01-07.** Cat MineStar Command for hauling was implemented at Luck Stone's Bull Run quarry in Chantilly, Virginia on a fleet of Cat 777 trucks. Caterpillar reports go-live in November 2024.

Source:  
https://www.caterpillar.com/en/news/caterpillarNews/2026/scaling-autonomy-system.html

This operating scope is specific:
- quarry/aggregates;
- Bull Run;
- Cat 777 fleet;
- single-shift operation in the reported first-year account.

It does not generalize to every mine/quarry, vehicle type, geography or shift schedule.

## Productive work and buyer job

**REPORTED FACT — Caterpillar.**
- shortly after go-live, productivity matched staffed machines;
- by July 2025, 1 million tons had been hauled autonomously;
- within the first year, over 2 million tons had been hauled at the single-shift operation;
- Caterpillar explains the buyer problem partly as operator scarcity: at a four-truck quarry, one truck without an available operator removes roughly one-quarter of that day's truck capacity.

This is materially stronger than an autonomous-distance headline because the observable is an economically relevant job: tons hauled in a quarry.

## Human/support burden

**REPORTED FACT — implementation was not labor-free.** Caterpillar and local dealer Carter Machinery embedded experts on site, implemented prerequisite technologies, supported change enablement, and helped define operating processes. Caterpillar says the quarry used fewer MineStar features than a large mining customer; the solution and training were simplified to reduce implementation time and cost.

This supports an important GMI distinction: autonomy can substitute for one operator bottleneck while still requiring site process redesign, dealer support, training and human oversight. "Driverless" is not "support-free."

## Retention / expansion signal

**REPORTED FACT — Caterpillar release dated 2026-09-15.** After more than 18 months of autonomous hauling at Bull Run, Caterpillar reported more than 3.5 million tons hauled and announced expansion with Luck Stone to two additional Virginia operations, Boscobel and Bealeton, using autonomous-haulage fleets of Cat 775 trucks.

Source:  
https://www.caterpillar.com/en/news/corporate-press-releases/h/luck-stone-builds-on-autonomous-hauling-success-with-caterpillar.html

**INTERPRETATION.** Expansion to additional sites after a long operating interval is a stronger retention/adoption signal than an initial pilot agreement. It still does not reveal the customer's financial hurdle or contract price.

## Buyer and supplier economics: what is still missing

No inspected source discloses:
- Bull Run autonomy-system price or dealer/integration fees;
- incremental truck/retrofit capital attributed to autonomy;
- annual software/support fee;
- maintenance/spares delta versus staffed operation;
- remote-supervision labor hours;
- fuel/energy changes;
- insurance or safety-cost savings;
- quantified labor dollars saved;
- quarry revenue/margin uplift;
- Caterpillar autonomy revenue or margin for the contract.

Caterpillar's consolidated Resource Industries financials cannot be allocated to this project.

## Useful conclusion

The Bull Run evidence passes several commercial-adoption gates:
- real buyer and site;
- defined application;
- actual go-live;
- measured productive output over more than a year;
- productivity compared with staffed machines;
- implementation/support burden acknowledged;
- subsequent multi-site expansion.

It therefore qualifies as a real operating autonomy case rather than a demo.

### Strongest competing explanation

The expansion may reflect strategic partnership value, workforce/safety goals or favorable pilot economics that are not representative of a fully priced mature commercial offering. Because project price and operating-cost data are absent, the observed production success does not by itself prove customer financial payback or Caterpillar margin.

### Next discriminating observation

Acquire a customer/dealer or contractual source for:
- autonomy software/hardware/dealer implementation price;
- staffed versus autonomous operator/supervisor FTEs;
- maintenance/availability;
- fuel/consumables;
- tons per operating hour or truck-hour;
- annualized operating cost;
- expansion contract terms.

The economically decisive unit is `cost per accepted ton hauled` for a matched operating scope, not autonomous miles or tons alone.

# Cross-case synthesis

The three cases expose three different commercialization mechanisms:

| Case | Physical/technical capability | Productive-work evidence | Revenue mechanism | Support/capital burden | Main missing economic link |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Symbotic/Walmart | warehouse system + software | operational-system cohort and buyer network automation | system deployment revenue + software maintenance + operations services | integration, training, spares, warranty, buyer capex | site-level throughput/labor/capital payback |
| EVgo public network | owned/operated fast-charging stalls | 99 GWh delivered; 276 kWh/day per public stall | paid charging + OEM/network + credits | energy, rent, maintenance; depreciation in profit, capex in cash investment; separate debt/subsidy | actual uptime, integrated exposure and site/vintage return |
| Cat/Luck Stone | autonomous haulage at defined quarry | >3.5m tons after go-live and expansion | equipment/technology/dealer commercial relationship; exact project revenue undisclosed | embedded support, process change, training, assets | matched cost/ton and supplier contract economics |

A common deployment chain emerges:

`capability -> contracted/financed installation -> accepted operation -> measured productive unit -> customer economic value -> supplier/operator revenue -> support + capital + financing -> retained cash`

Every arrow needs evidence. Skipping from catalog capability to theme membership, or from installed units to profitable adoption, is precisely the error this lane is designed to prevent.
