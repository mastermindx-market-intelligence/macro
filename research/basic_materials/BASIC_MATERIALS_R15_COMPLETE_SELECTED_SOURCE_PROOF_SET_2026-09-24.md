## 3. Customer acceptance should not become a one-number maturity score

The proposed explanation keeps several questions distinct:

1. What did the source report about the tested material?
2. Which observer performed which test under which population?
3. Did the customer accept the relevant product/process, or merely report measurements?
4. Is there an executed commercial commitment or shipment?
5. Does repeat activity generate cash after manufacturing, support and funding needs?

These are investigative questions, not a mandatory universal order of business events. Different contracts may arrange milestones differently. The system should not invent a stage transition absent source evidence.

A test-count ratio answers only an arithmetic question about the disclosed count. It does not identify the commercial importance of each criterion, dependence between criteria, cost of remediation, retesting burden or likelihood of eventual success. The economically decisive question may be the single remaining condition, not the fraction already met.

The internal/customer discrepancy is also useful information. The correct synthesis is not to average the results or automatically prefer the favorable one. Preserve attribution and ask what matched testing, customer acknowledgment or process evidence would resolve the discrepancy.

There is a similar mistake at the next stage: a completed acceptance may still leave questions about orders, utilization, yield, price and funding. Conversely, absent disclosure of a milestone is not proof that it failed. A missing field must stay unknown, while a specific reported unmet criterion stays a reported observation in its own dated scope.

## 4. Contractual economics: handle the threshold within the period

The prior research distinguished the high-share and low-share phases. R15 adds a more demanding example: the contractual threshold is crossed partway through the modeled period.

Assume, strictly for illustration:

- 10 million ounces of mine silver before the stated fixed payable factor;
- an illustrative constant silver price of US$30 per ounce;
- only the BHP agreement, with no other stream interests included;
- fractional delivery allocation and a marginal switch at the threshold;
- no tax, financing, overhead, investment recovery or settlement timing effects.

The payable input is 9 million ounces. The 33.75% percentage already expresses the initial share of that defined input; multiplying it by a second 33.75% ownership factor would be an error. Likewise, an input already measured as payable silver must not receive the 90% factor a second time.

### Three explicit scenarios

| Assumed delivery position at period start | Delivered silver in the scenario | Contribution after the stated ongoing payment |
|---|---:|---:|
| Before the threshold, with enough high-share capacity for the whole period | 3.0375 million oz | US$72.9 million |
| Threshold already met | 2.025 million oz | US$48.6 million |
| 98.9875 million oz already delivered under this agreement | 2.3625 million oz | US$56.7 million |

All quantities and results in this table are modeled, not actual Wheaton production, receipts or reported contribution. The last column is deliberately not free cash flow, fair value or return on the original investment.

### Crossing-case arithmetic

The assumed opening counter leaves 1.0125 million high-share ounces to be delivered before the threshold:

`100,000,000 - 98,987,500 = 1,012,500`.

At the initial share, those deliveries consume 3 million payable input ounces:

`1,012,500 / 0.3375 = 3,000,000`.

The remaining 6 million payable input ounces participate at the lower share:

`6,000,000 × 0.225 = 1,350,000`.

Total modeled deliveries are therefore 2.3625 million ounces. At the assumed price, gross modeled proceeds are US$70.875 million, the ongoing payment is US$14.175 million, and contribution before the stated exclusions is US$56.7 million.

Using the initial share for the whole period overstates this result by US$16.2 million. Using the lower share for the whole period understates it by US$8.1 million. These differences follow from the scenario assumptions; they are not identified errors in a company report.

### Unknown does not mean zero

The actual agreement-specific delivery counter has not been obtained. Consequently, the worksheet does not choose a current phase. It can present labeled scenarios, but it refuses an actual-phase result when the counter is missing.

A calendar date cannot supply the counter. Nor can aggregate Wheaton silver sales, all Antamina streams or total mine production substitute for the named agreement's delivered-ounce history.

Upfront investment is another independent question. The ongoing contribution formula does not say whether paying US$4.3 billion created value. That requires duration, production, price, counterparty, taxes, financing, contract details and timing on a consistent valuation basis. R3's financing and per-share analysis remain the governing research direction for that stronger capability.
