# 03 — External literature and mechanism census

## 1. What the evidence can support

The primary literature supports testing session seasonality, conditional liquidity provision, information-linked continuation, book-state imbalance and option-position scenarios as different mechanisms. It does not establish one transferable “low confirmed” score. Theory papers supply useful targets and evaluation tools; historical empirical papers provide bounded mechanisms and baseline designs. None is a new result for this estate.

This chapter records the study universe, dates, sampling grain, target horizon, validation design and limits wherever the consulted primary material supports them. Missing details are stated as missing. Older manuscripts are not silently substituted for final-paper numerical results. L01–L10, M01–M12 and W17–W25 are literature/source identifiers and retain the package register mappings.

**Experiment mapping is a proposal, not a launched wave:** R1 qualifies source/clock/observation contracts; R2 is the DEV-only support and session census; R3 establishes price, label and policy baselines; R4 tests technical families; R5 adds continuous L1; R6 challenges L1 with deeper book evidence; R7 adds options, relative and catalyst context. The frozen estimands, gates and same-candidate policy comparison in chapters 06, 08 and 09 control every later experiment.

## 2. Technical rules, periodicity, momentum and reversal

| Source | Universe, period, grain, horizon and method | Result, OOS status, limitation and first experiment |
|---|---|---|
| **L01** — [Andersen & Bollerslev (1997), *Intraday Periodicity and Volatility Persistence in Financial Markets*](https://finance.martinsewell.com/stylized-facts/volatility/AndersenBollerslev1997b.pdf) | Five-minute DM/USD, October 1992–September 1993 (74,880 observations), and S&P 500 futures, 1986–1989 (79,280; specified 1987 crash interval excluded). Models intraday volatility periodicity and persistence, not first-passage direction. | Strong time-of-day volatility patterns coexist with weak mean-return seasonality: only 13/288 FX intervals breach the nominal 5% mean-return band after varying variance. No LLR-style executable out-of-sample policy is established. **R2/R3:** freeze clock/volatility seasonal baselines and elapsed-time strata; do not give volatility seasonality a directional sign or transfer these old futures/FX samples to 24H equities. |
| **L02** — [Heston, Korajczyk & Sadka (2010), *Intraday Patterns in the Cross-section of Stock Returns*](https://www.bauer.uh.edu/departments/finance/documents/Heston_Korajczyk_Sadka_paper_UH.pdf) | May 2010 author manuscript: 1,715 NYSE firms, 2001–2005 TAQ, thirteen half-hour intervals; separate 1993–2000 tick-size robustness. Cross-sectional sorts examine same-clock continuation and nearby-interval reversal. | Continuation recurs at the same clock interval over at least 40 trading days; sub-hour reversals include bid–ask effects. Immediate bid/ask execution makes the reported strategy means negative across size/time groups, with one-way cost estimates around 4–7bp. **R3/R4:** compare clock-matched baselines and actionable prices. A state-space model separates latent efficient value from inventory-related transitory pressure; relevant decay is measured in days. | Average pressure is about 49bp with a 0.92-day half-life; largest-stock estimates are 17bp/0.54 day, smallest 118bp/2.11 days. This is structural estimation using inventory, not a frozen OHLCV entry forecast. **R5/R7:** distinguish inventory compensation from informed continuation and stratify liquidity. Neither the private inventory input nor the estimated half-life can be reconstructed from a candle or exported to a minutes-long target. |
| **L04** — [Nagel (2012), *Evaporating Liquidity*](https://bpb-us-w2.wpmucdn.com/voices.uchicago.edu/dist/f/575/files/2020/07/LiqSupply.pdf) | December 2011 manuscript: CRSP NYSE/AMEX/Nasdaq, 1998–2010, daily reversal portfolios using prior five-day returns and 48 industries. Relates compensation to VIX; a pre-June-2007 fit is applied to the crisis period. | High-stress reversal compensation increases even in large/liquid groups. Monthly adjusted fit is about 56% with transaction returns versus 25% with midquotes, exposing the importance of price measurement. The later-period application is useful regime evidence, not per-stock low calibration. **R3/R5:** predeclare stress and liquidity interactions, and measure costs. Strategy compensation, conditional on supplying liquidity, is different from certainty that a shock has ended. |
| **L05** — [Gao, Han, Li & Zhou (2018), *Market Intraday Momentum*](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/market-intraday-momentum/) | SPY TAQ, February 1993–December 2013. Regresses 15:30–16:00 return on the first interval, defined from the previous close to 10:00; thus the predictor includes overnight movement. Published record and October 2014 full draft were consulted. | Published headline fit is approximately R²=1.6%; effects strengthen with volatility, volume and important news. The older draft reports out-of-sample tests, whose exact figures are not represented as final-paper results here. **R3/R7:** use this as an index/time-of-day continuation comparator. It is not a generic opening-half-hour signal, a single-stock reversal rule, or evidence that an overnight equity venue shares regular-session dynamics. |
| **L09** — [Savor (2012), *Stock Returns After Major Price Shocks: The Impact of Information*](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2012/10/Stock-Returns-After-Major-Price-Shocks---May-2012---Final.pdf) | 1995–2009: 166,470 >10% absolute four-factor abnormal-return events; eligible stocks need at least five analyst reports in the prior year. Classifies information using reports on days −1, 0 and +1; examines 5–40-day drift/reversal. | Information-linked moves tend to drift; no-report events tend to reverse. This is historical classification, not an as-of shock detector: tomorrow's report cannot enter today's features. **R7:** retain publication/receipt cutoffs and explicit analyst-coverage controls. A generic no-headline flag does not reproduce the study; the results neither revive the killed OHLCV/news classifier nor establish 30-minute executable alpha. |
| **M09** — [Sullivan, Timmermann & White (1999), *Data-Snooping, Technical Trading Rule Performance, and the Bootstrap*](https://www.fmg.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2020-11/dp303.pdf) | 1998 working paper underlying the 1999 publication: 7,846 technical rules on daily DJIA, 1897–1996; later-period 1987–1996 comparison and S&P 500 futures, 1984–1996. Uses full-family bootstrap assessment. | Some historical favorable results survive adjustment; the ten-year post-sample experiment does not reproduce them, and the futures exercise finds no outperforming-rule evidence. **R4:** compare complete preregistered technical families on later dates and economic costs. The result is neither a universal rejection of technical measurements nor evidence for today's intraday rules. Searching indicators independently and retaining only winners defeats the lesson. |

## 3. Order flow, queue state, replenishment and execution

| Source | Universe, period, grain, horizon and method | Result, OOS status, limitation and first experiment |
|---|---|---|
| **L06** — [Cont, Kukanov & Stoikov (2014), *The Price Impact of Order Book Events*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1011.6402) | March 2011 manuscript: 50 randomly selected S&P 500 stocks in April 2010 TAQ, using best prices/sizes and trades; primary bins are ten seconds with timestamps rounded to seconds. Regresses same-bin price changes on event-defined OFI. | Average explanatory R² is about 65%, with impact slope related inversely to depth. | **L07** — [Gould & Bonart (2016), *Queue Imbalance as a One-Tick-Ahead Price Predictor in a Limit Order Book*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1512.03492) | Ten Nasdaq stocks over 252 trading days in 2014, LOBSTER single-venue book data, 10:00–15:30; 100 observations per stock/day with a random 80/20 within-year split. Logistic models predict the direction of the next mid-price change. | AUC is roughly 0.70–0.80 for large-tick and 0.60–0.65 for small-tick stocks. **R5/R6:** book imbalance is a plausible short-event-horizon challenger with tick-size interactions. Random splits are not a future-year or session-transfer test; the paper does not establish net execution profit, consolidated-market coverage, or a 30-minute low probability. |
| **L08** — [Stoikov (2018), *The Micro-Price: A High-Frequency Estimator of Future Prices*](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/14697688.2018.1489139) | Published abstract plus the author's presentation were available; full article retrieval was not. Slides use BAC/CVX quotes from March 2011, fit the first day, and show three subsequent days and month-level 1/5/10-minute comparisons. | The micro-price is a recursively corrected expected-mid estimate under a Markov model; a simple size-weighted midpoint is only a special construction. No numerical gain is imported from inaccessible full text. **R5/R6:** compare a precisely defined weighted-mid baseline and any fitted correction on frozen later data. Model horizon-independence is not evidence of universal empirical accuracy or an executable-fill forecast. |
| **L10** — [Degryse, de Jong, van Ravenswaaij & Wuyts (2005), *Aggressive Orders and the Resiliency of a Limit Order Market*](https://academic.oup.com/rof/article-abstract/9/2/201/1611889) | July 2002 manuscript: 20 Paris stocks, February 23–August 24, 1998, 123 days; event study of orders and best limits. | Published findings distinguish spread/depth recovery within roughly 20 updates from price recovery: initial reversal can be followed by renewed movement in the initial impact direction. **R6:** label depth, spread, flow and price separately, on event and elapsed-time clocks. No universal decay constant or OOS low-timing rule is established; replenishment does not prove that information-driven selling is exhausted. |
| **M10** — [Zhang, Zohren & Roberts (2019), *DeepLOB*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1808.03668) | FI2010 has five Nordic stocks/ten days; the larger 2017 LSE study has five liquid names, ten levels per side and >134 million samples, with six training, three validation and three test months. Targets are 10–100 book events, depending on dataset. | CNN/Inception/LSTM results include LSE 20-event F1=70.15% and 68.48% on five unseen stocks. **R6:** a later architecture challenger only after book admission and simpler baselines. Event-count mid-price classes, short FI2010 history and simplified trading do not establish cost-adjusted hour-scale first-passage usefulness, overnight transfer, or accessible historical L2 data in this estate. |
| **M11** — [Sirignano & Cont (2019), *Universal Features of Price Formation in Financial Markets*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.06917) | Approximately 1,000 Nasdaq stocks, January 2014–March 2017, with order, cancellation and trade events. Pooled recurrent models target the next mid-price change, commonly fractions of a second to seconds; tests include later periods and unseen stocks. | The pooled LSTM learns shared predictive structure and outperforms asset-specific comparators in the studied setting. **R6:** pool across securities only with held-out names/dates and explicit scale normalization; compare against transparent book-state models. The paper's “universal” description does not establish 24H stationarity, modern venue transfer, a causal mechanism, net profits, or equivalent information in OHLCV and signed-volume proxies. |
| **W25** — [Almgren & Chriss (2000 manuscript; 2001 publication), *Optimal Execution of Portfolio Transactions*](https://smallake.kr/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/optliq.pdf) | Analytical execution framework and numerical examples, using idealized price dynamics and temporary/permanent impact. The horizon is a chosen liquidation schedule, not a forecast of the next low; there is no LLR empirical universe or out-of-sample alpha test. | The cost–risk frontier makes delay and impact competing objectives. **R3 onward:** compare all-candidate common-terminal wealth, fill uncertainty and exposure under each policy; fit any costs to admitted data. The theory cannot supply current spreads, queue fills, a retail optimal schedule, or a price forecast. Partial fills and missed orders still require explicit policy accounting. |

## 5. Survival, distributions, calibration and selection control

These methodological sources do not predict a financial effect size. Their role is to define what a claim means and how a new result could be evaluated. The package's concrete CI inequalities, practical-effect bars, censoring treatment and supported-cell rules are design commitments, not numbers derived from these papers.

| Source | Universe, period, grain, horizon and method | Result, OOS status, limitation and first experiment |
|---|---|---|
| **M01** — [Fine & Gray (1999), *A Proportional Hazards Model for the Subdistribution of a Competing Risk*](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1999.10474144) | Statistical theory, simulations and a breast-cancer trial; no financial universe or market horizon. The original reproduced article text was inspected after publisher full-text retrieval failed. Models covariates in cumulative incidence under competing events. | **R3:** distinguish target-first cumulative probability by H from a cause-specific instantaneous rate and from fixed-window support survival. Treat stop-first as an observed competing event, not ordinary loss to follow-up. The paper does not establish proportional hazards or independent censoring for markets, or calibrate any LLR probability. A competing-risk model cannot substitute for an exact event-order label. |
| **M02** — [Koenker & Bassett (1978), *Regression Quantiles*](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~jordan/sail/readings/koenker-bassett.pdf) | Foundational estimation theory for conditional regression quantiles via asymmetric absolute loss, with no trading sample, deployment horizon or market OOS claim. The consulted original-paper PDF was scanned; bibliographic and first-page access were available. | **R3:** use transparent quantile baselines for a specifically named return or path functional, alongside point and distributional comparators. Pinball loss estimates a conditional quantile; it does not produce a guaranteed stop, one coherent joint path, or simultaneous first-passage probabilities from separate marginal predictions. Quantile ordering, support and calibration therefore remain protocol questions, not inherited empirical facts. |
| **M03** — [Gneiting & Raftery (2007), *Strictly Proper Scoring Rules, Prediction, and Estimation*](https://sites.stat.washington.edu/people/raftery/Research/PDF/Gneiting2007jasa.pdf) | General probability-forecast theory with weather illustrations, not a financial strategy or a particular market horizon. Examines proper probability/distribution scores, including log/Brier, continuous ranked probability and interval scoring. | **R3 onward:** compare frozen forecasts with proper scores on the same eligible outcomes, while separately checking reliability and policy utility. Accuracy or AUC alone cannot establish probability calibration; broad intervals may cover without useful sharpness. The source supplies scoring principles, not numerical pass thresholds or financial OOS success. Those thresholds, intervals and estimable cohorts must be fixed in the protocol. |
| **M04** — [Romano, Patterson & Candès (2019), *Conformalized Quantile Regression*](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/8613-conformalized-quantile-regression.pdf) | Synthetic heteroskedastic settings and eleven regression datasets across 2,200 experiments with random splits. Split CQR fits conditional quantiles and adjusts them using separate conformity scores; the theorem requires exchangeability. | Finite-sample coverage is marginal; with distinct scores its upper slack is 1/(n_cal+1). There is no chronological stock-market or 24H transfer test. **R3:** retain a separate calibration segment and report realized coverage by supported session/horizon cells. Overlapping paths and regime drift do not automatically satisfy the theorem, and global coverage does not guarantee conditional or simultaneous path coverage. |
| **M05** — [Guo, Pleiss, Sun & Weinberger (2017), *On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks*](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v70/guo17a/guo17a.pdf) | Image/text classification benchmarks including CIFAR, ImageNet and SVHN; no market horizon. Compares held-out calibration methods, with temperature scaling often effective. One CIFAR100 ResNet110 stochastic-depth example improves ECE from 12.67% to 0.96%. | The numbers are benchmark-specific, not expected financial gains. **R3 onward:** fit any calibrator on a separate chronological slice and disclose reliability-bin support/uncertainty. Test whether calibration improvements survive a frozen later period, rather than interpreting classification discrimination as reliability. Thin overnight cells, drift and richer calibrator overfit can invalidate simple transfer; unsupported cells need explicit pooling or abstention. |
| **M06** — [Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu (2015 manuscript; 2017 publication), *The Probability of Backtest Overfitting*](https://www.davidhbailey.com/dhbpapers/backtest-prob.pdf) | Strategy-selection performance matrices with methodological, simulation and application examples; no LLR dataset/horizon. Combinatorially symmetric cross-validation estimates how often the in-sample selected strategy ranks below the out-of-sample median. | That probability is about selection instability, not a losing trade. **All waves:** retain attempted and failed alternatives, and apply the diagnostic only where sample size and dependence assumptions are defensible. Reused time blocks do not replace a forward deployment simulation; selection analysis does not repair a leaked forecasting model. The paper supplies no automatic authorization for adding trials or selecting a production winner. |
| **M07** — [Bailey & López de Prado (2014), *The Deflated Sharpe Ratio*](https://www.davidhbailey.com/dhbpapers/deflated-sharpe.pdf) | Analytical and numerical treatment of Sharpe selection, sample length and non-normality; no new market strategy or fixed forecast horizon. Adjusts performance significance for selection among multiple trials and non-Gaussian returns. | **Policy stage:** apply only to a declared net-return series with exposure, costs, concurrency and cash conventions. It is not a confidence measure for classifier AUC, a per-entry probability, or proof that an interval forecast is calibrated. Actual attempted trials and dependence matter; hiding failed variants defeats the adjustment. Chronological confirmation and separate risk gates remain necessary design requirements. |
| **M08** — [White (2000), *A Reality Check for Data Snooping*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1468-0262.00152) | Publisher abstract of the September 2000 Econometrica paper. Develops a bootstrap test for repeated specification search on dependent histories: whether the best encountered model has predictive superiority over a specified benchmark. No LLR study or session-level OOS result is supplied. | **All waves:** define the candidate family and benchmark, preserve the complete search, and use defensible dependence resampling. A lone significant feature after many alternatives is not sufficient evidence. The method addresses the search problem; it cannot undo future information, incorrect known-times, a changing evaluation cohort, or a post hoc utility threshold. |
| **M12** — [López de Prado, author inventory of AFML methods](https://www.quantresearch.org/Innovations.htm) | The author's method inventory documents purging, embargo and combinatorial purged cross-validation associated with the 2018 book. This is provenance for method names, not a consulted full-book empirical replication or a dataset-specific OOS result. | **All waves:** set overlap handling from each actual information/outcome interval and preserve later chronological tests. No universal embargo percentage, financial edge or automatic independence guarantee is inferred from the inventory. Nearby candidate rows may share events and labels even if their timestamps differ; protection must address those intervals and shared sessions, not merely row distance. |

## 6. Admission order and useful first comparisons

| Research question | First proposed comparison | What would count as an unearned transfer |
|---|---|---|
| Does technical state add information? | R4, against the identical price/volatility/time-of-day baseline and cohort; retain complete family accounting | Treating a historical rule winner, next-tick classifier, or present chart appearance as a current probability |
| Is a reversal a liquidity response? | R5, ordered pre-cutoff trade/quote observations with information, price-path and volatility controls | Calling an OHLCV wick forced liquidation or treating a future report as already known |
| Does replenishment help beyond L1? | R6, deeper-book versus L1 on identical names/dates, with cost, latency and missingness | Equating restored displayed depth with restored efficient value or guaranteed queue execution |
| Do options or catalysts add value? | R7, each family separately, then a small registered interaction set and remove-one-family comparisons | Public OI as participant inventory; stock/index or daily/intraday equivalence; hindsight catalyst classification |
| Is the prediction useful for entry? | All-candidate common-terminal policy wealth with fill/partial-fill/cash accounting, plus separate risk and calibration gates | Comparing selected filled winners only, importing gross paper returns, or using discrimination as economic utility |

The logical sequence is observation admission → explicit target → frozen baseline → matched incremental test → supported calibration → executable policy comparison. Source unavailability, insufficient power, a statistically unresolved result and a tested failure remain different outcomes. External mechanisms do not cancel the stable internal kills in chapter 02.
