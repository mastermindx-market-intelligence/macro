## 1. Central conclusion: capacity, revenue and retained value must be modeled separately

The user needs to understand which technology demand is growing, which physical architecture serves it, what each supplier actually contributes, and how much value survives purchased inputs, execution risk, financing and valuation. A generic AI-hardware basket does not answer these questions.

The proposed economic chain is:

`customer workload and budget -> selected architecture -> qualified configuration -> ordered equipment -> complete usable systems -> accepted deployment -> useful operating capacity -> supplier revenue and retained profit -> cash and reinvestment -> expectations and valuation`

This is an analytical view, not a replacement event/lifecycle system. These events do not always occur in a strict universal order: revenue recognition depends on the contract, financing and acceptance terms; services can begin before or after physical delivery; a customer can reserve capacity before qualification. Preserve native source meanings rather than force every company into this drawing.

Three distinct quantities govern the model: useful capacity, chargeable product/service units, and retained economic value. A faster interconnect can reduce the number of modules required for the same capacity. More expensive purchased components can increase a systems vendor's revenue without increasing gross profit. A design-rich platform can earn more margin but require additional engineering and working capital. Inventory drawdown can coexist with improving final demand.

These are hypotheses and accounting/physical constraints to investigate, not a blanket forecast for physical Technology. Supplier scarcity, customer adoption, revenue growth, earnings revisions and price leadership require separate evidence.

## 6. Demand conversion and bottleneck migration

A bottleneck claim needs an object, location, application/configuration, time interval, demand basis, qualified available output, substitution conditions, and economic consequence. Nameplate capacity, yield-adjusted output, customer-qualified capacity and available-for-this-customer supply are different quantities. The model must preserve all of them when disclosed and explicitly lack them when not.

The useful question is not merely 'what component is scarce?' It is 'what currently limits the delivery of a complete accepted system, and where does the limiting constraint move after that improves?'

For example, additional optical modules need not increase system delivery if thermal integration or testing is limiting. Additional manufacturing capacity can be unhelpful until the customer qualifies it. More qualified output can increase unit volumes while reducing scarcity pricing. A customer can change architecture to bypass a constraint, transferring the value pool rather than simply enlarging it.

Do not infer a bottleneck from backlog alone. Backlog can reflect contract duration, delivery scheduling, reservations, pricing, order-definition changes or demand. Book-to-bill compares monetary flows; it is not a lead-time, utilization or yield series. Customer and supplier announcements about the same program must remain one linked evidence family rather than several independent confirmations.

For a physical system, a simple complete-kit upper bound is the minimum of compatible qualified quantities divided by required content, further limited by assembly, test and customer acceptance capacity. This is useful as a transparent illustrative calculation. It is not a general factory simulator: alternative configurations, common resources, rework, stochastic yield, batching, time offsets, logistics and priority allocations can require a more detailed model under existing owners.

## 7. Spending and value capture without double counting

Keep three views synchronized but not merged into one additive total:

- **Physical contents:** what is contained within a configuration.
- **Commercial transfers:** who purchases what from whom, at a defined purchase boundary.
- **Retained economics:** revenue less relevant bought-in content and delivery costs, with capital and financing separately identified.

If a customer buys a rack from a systems vendor, that vendor pays a manufacturer, and the manufacturer purchases components, the three revenues are different transfers along the chain. Adding them produces turnover across stages, not final demand or value added. A total-market chart must name the economic boundary and eliminate overlap; otherwise display an unweighted relationship map.

This also changes valuation analysis. Gross-profit growth can be more informative than top-line growth for a pass-through-heavy configuration, but gross profit itself is not free cash flow. A design-rich platform may deserve a different peer analysis from build-to-print manufacturing only if the evidence supports retained design value, durability and the required reinvestment. An attractive operating margin can coexist with poor returns if funding needs and capital intensity rise sufficiently.

For parent assemblies and included parts, preserve one selected purchase boundary before cost or quantity aggregation. A module's complete price cannot be added to the prices of the parts already included in it. A two-ended link count needs two compatible endpoint quantities only if the design actually uses that configuration. Redundancy, spares and oversubscription assumptions belong in the record, not hidden in visualization widths.

## 9. Five original physical/financial scenario families

These are hypothetical mechanism tests, not actual supplier quotes, customer configurations, shortage measurements or stock forecasts. Their inputs and arithmetic are retained in the calculation artifact and described completely here.

### 9.1 Purchased-content inflation: revenue +35%, gross profit unchanged

Assume 100 systems annually. Each initially sells for $1,000 and contains $700 of purchased parts and $100 of conversion costs. Annual revenue is $100,000, cost is $80,000, gross profit is $20,000 and gross margin is 20%.

Purchased content rises to $1,050, fully passed through to a new $1,350 price. Conversion cost and unit count are unchanged. Revenue rises to $135,000; cost becomes $115,000. Gross profit remains $20,000 and gross margin falls to about 14.8148%.

Now assume a simplified 360-day annual model with 30 days of receivables on sales, 45 days of inventory on cost and 30 days of payables on cost. Modeled working capital rises from about $11,666.67 to $16,041.67, an additional $4,375. These constant timing assumptions are illustrative, not an issuer's inventory accounting method.

The lesson is not that pass-through growth is inherently bad. It is that top-line scale, retained profit, margin percentage and financing needs can move differently. Interest, overhead, taxes and reinvestment would require further bridges.

### 9.2 Faster links: required capacity +50%, selected module revenue +5%

Assume an idealized required aggregate capacity of 80 Tbps served by 0.8-Tbps links, with two one-module endpoints per link and $1,000 per module. That gives 100 links, 200 modules and $200,000 of selected module revenue.

Required capacity becomes 120 Tbps and link capacity becomes 1.6 Tbps. At $1,400 per module, the simplified design uses 75 links and 150 modules, producing $210,000 of selected revenue. Required capacity rose 50%, module count fell 25% and selected revenue rose 5%.

The illustration ignores topology, overhead, redundancy, utilization, latency and repair requirements. It is not a real deployment design. A new CPO/NPO/OCS or other architecture would require a new purchased-object boundary rather than silently reusing the module equation.

### 9.3 Qualified-capacity constraint migration

For one common interval and a fictional configuration, 1,400 qualified optical units at 14 per system support 100 systems; 280 qualified boards at two per system support 140; assembly supports 100; test supports 90; thermal integration supports 80. The simple complete-system upper bound is 80.

Doubling only optical capacity leaves the limit at 80. Raising thermal capacity to 100 moves the limit to 90, now testing. Raising test to 110 while thermal is 100 moves the bound to 100, where optics and assembly bind. This is why a supplier's capacity announcement may not increase the end system's delivery rate.

Quantities are already qualified/usable and share a compatible period/configuration. Do not apply an additional arbitrary yield factor or treat this minimum model as a stochastic production forecast.

### 9.4 Channel inventory: upstream shipments down while final demand rises

Start with 40 comparable units in channel inventory. Ship 130 and sell 100 to end customers: closing inventory is 70. Next period ship 90 and sell 110: closing inventory is 50. End demand grew 10% while upstream shipments fell approximately 30.7692%.

This assumes no returns, transfers, scrapping or other flows. It demonstrates a possible destocking pattern; it does not establish that any current vendor is in one. Monetary inventory, reported shipments, sell-in and sell-through with different scopes cannot be substituted into the equation without reconciliation.

### 9.5 Earnings rise while capital productivity falls

Assume normalized after-tax operating earnings rise from 10 to 12, while comparable average invested capital rises from 50 to 100 over equal full periods. Earnings rise 20%; the earnings-to-average-capital rate falls from 20% to 12%. The change in earnings divided by the change in capital is 4%.

This simplified incremental ratio is not a causal project return or an issuer's published ROIC. Real investments have ramp lags, risk and option value. The example explains why the valuation model needs the timing and return on required capital, not only current profit growth.

The seven scenario families counted for the combined R3 laboratory are these five plus R3A's contract-pricing and equal-delivery TCV examples. They do not create new trading signals.

## 10. Rerating, expectations and leadership hypotheses

The research should test a sequence of falsifiable questions rather than fit one universal hardware score.

**Demand quality:** is end use growing, or is the observed change pricing, richer configuration, channel stocking, contract timing or perimeter? Seek compatible quantities, customer spending and definition-vintage evidence.

**Content and share:** does the supplier retain more qualified content per useful system? Investigate customer design choice, architecture substitution and competing qualified sources. Company marketing does not establish a monopoly or market share.

**Execution and conversion:** can the supplier deliver a complete accepted configuration on time? Distinguish component availability from system-level test/thermal/installation limits and customer readiness.

**Retained profit:** do mix, price and utilization improve earnings after purchased components, conversion costs, warranty, integration and support? Keep reported and adjusted measures and their bridges separate.

**Cash and capital:** does growth require inventory, receivables, customer financing, factories or leases? Record customer advances and payable financing without assuming they remain available through a downturn. Evaluate replacement investment and the ramp of new capacity.

**Expectations and valuation:** compare the same metric and business perimeter across management guidance, available dated consensus and scenario assumptions.
