## 1. The investment question that changes the model

Technology services do not earn money merely because technology adoption rises. The relevant question is: what paid responsibility does the provider assume, how does it charge, how much human and nonhuman delivery cost remains, and who captures any efficiency gain at the next contract negotiation?

A customer can spend more on modernization while reducing its legacy operations bill. A consultant can win a large contract but begin recognizing revenue only after mobilization. An automation provider can reduce billable hours while improving fixed-fee margins. An acquisition can increase headcount and reported sales without indicating new organic customer demand. These are analytical possibilities to test, not predictions for the companies below.

The native chain should be:

`buyer need and funded budget -> scoped commitment -> mobilization -> accepted delivery or continuing service -> recognized revenue -> retained contribution -> billing and collection -> renewal/repricing`

This is a research view over existing owners, not a new lifecycle engine. Pipeline, signings, bookings, total contract value, accounting remaining obligations, backlog, recognized revenue and collected cash remain different observations. The model must permit unavailable links rather than infer all later stages from the first announcement.

## 3. Contract economics: the same automation can have opposite revenue effects

### 3.1 Time-and-materials

A simple project bridge is `billable hours x realized rate`. Delivery cost includes compensated hours, subcontractors, cloud/model use, project overhead, training and rework. Paid utilization must use the disclosed eligible workforce and time denominator; total employees are not billable employees.

Higher productivity can lower hours required for an unchanged customer outcome. This can lower project revenue unless rates rise, new scope grows, freed capacity is sold or the contract changes. Conversely, inadequate skills or delays can increase nonbillable time even during apparently strong demand. Do not interpret lower headcount as either productivity success or demand failure without perimeter, mix and delivery evidence.

### 3.2 Fixed-price and fixed-scope projects

Price is agreed for a specified deliverable. Efficiency can increase contribution, but estimation errors, acceptance delays, change control, warranties and remediation can reverse the benefit. Recognition of revenue follows the issuer's accounting policy, not the research formula for project economics. A signed amount is not automatically revenue in the quarter of signing.

### 3.3 Managed services and minimum commitments

Separate base scope, variable consumption, service-level obligations, escalation/de-escalation clauses, transition costs and renewal pricing. A more profitable renewal with lower revenue is different from a lost customer. Customer cost savings and vendor margin gains can coexist, but their split depends on the contract and competition.

### 3.4 Outcome-based contracts

Define the accepted outcome, attribution, baseline, quality and rework. A generated answer or completed automation run is not necessarily an accepted business outcome. Pricing can be fixed, usage-linked, minimum-plus-variable or gain-sharing; the marketing term outcome-based does not identify a unique accounting model.

### 3.5 Platform or resale attachment

Separate services revenue from product licenses, cloud resale and third-party costs. A partnership can expand the provider's capability or distribution without giving it ownership of the platform's economics. Gross revenue, net commission and retained contribution must not be conflated. The existing financial owner, not the research browser, owns principal/agent accounting interpretation.

## 4. Company cases: reported facts versus proposed inference

All source dates and locators appear in section 10. Figures are dated disclosed observations, not September live run rates. Company claims about demand and strategy remain attributed; the mechanisms and falsifiers are this research's proposals.

### Accenture — transformation demand versus conversion and business perimeter

The June 18 release for the quarter ended May 31, 2026 reports new bookings of $19.3 billion, revenue of $18.7 billion and local-currency revenue growth of 3%, versus 6% in reported dollars [S01]. These rounded figures are not a same-cohort conversion ratio.

Research the split between new project scope, renewals, acquisitions and currency; the buyer's discretionary versus operational budget; mobilization and delivery; and the scope of AI-enabled versus separately sold AI work. Hypothesis: integration accountability and industry process knowledge can support sustained paid scope. Falsifier: large deal announcements do not improve organic revenue, realized rates or cash after transition costs. Its already-researched historical AI bookings/revenue definitions in R1 must not be silently replaced with a broader current label.

### TCS — annualized AI measures versus quarterly total economics

TCS's July 9 release for the quarter ended June 30, 2026 reports quarterly revenue of $7.624 billion, total contract value of $9.5 billion and annualized AI revenue of $2.6 billion [S02]. The annualized measure is not $2.6 billion of quarterly AI revenue. The source also marks certain margin measures as excluding one-offs; that basis must survive comparisons.

Study outsourcing share, cloud/data/cyber modernization, delivery mix and the value retained after customer productivity commitments. Falsifier: annualized AI growth primarily reclassifies existing work or does not translate into comparable total revenue and contribution. Do not infer a precise AI revenue share until the annualization method and numerator/denominator scope are compatible.

### Infosys — net-new deal scope, acquired expertise and buyer identity

The issuer's July 23 release for the June 2026 quarter reports large-deal total contract value of $3.6 billion, with 61% net new, and AI revenue at 8.2% of total revenue under its disclosed presentation [S03]. The percentage and deal category do not establish universal comparability with another provider's AI or booking definitions.

Research how incremental deal scope converts into paid implementation and ongoing services. Preserve acquired expertise, organically built capability and the actual owner of each product. A separately documented example is the April 23 completion of the Stratus acquisition; the source identifies insurance technology expertise [S09]. The completion date matters for business-to-issuer mapping; it does not quantify acquired contribution to a theme.

### Cognizant — broad period strength can coexist with a weaker latest quarter

Cognizant's July 29 release reports trailing-twelve-month bookings up 5% to $29.1 billion, while second-quarter bookings declined 6% year over year. It separately defines large deals as total contract value of at least $100 million [S04]. Therefore a single directionless bookings-growth field is insufficient.

Study the share of new scope, signing cohorts, duration, acquired businesses and conversion into delivered work. Hypothesis: industry-specific implementation and engineering can win paid scope as clients move beyond experiments. Falsifier: growth relies on longer renewals or acquisition scope while comparable forward demand and realized pricing weaken. Neither one quarter nor a trailing aggregate alone adjudicates this hypothesis.

### EPAM — engineering capacity is not the total employee count

EPAM's August 6 release reports Q2 revenue growth of 4.5%, or 3.4% on its organic constant-currency definition, and approximately 56,650 delivery professionals within approximately 62,850 total employees [S05]. Its organic definition removes acquired-company revenue for the first twelve months and translates currency under a specified method.

Study client product-development budgets, specialist capability, outsourcing share, utilization, delivery location and subcontractor mix. Hypothesis: complex engineering and accountable production work can retain value after code generation becomes cheaper. Falsifier: customer program cancellations, lower realized billable demand or price compression overwhelm productivity. Revenue divided by closing total headcount is at most a crude descriptive proxy, not realized hourly productivity.

### Kyndryl — signatures are estimates; the newest window and trailing window differ

Kyndryl reports quarterly signings of $3.9 billion versus $3.2 billion, but trailing signings of $14.2 billion versus $18.3 billion. It defines signings as an initial estimate of customer commitment, involving contract duration and termination considerations, and warns they are not a comprehensive future-revenue measure [S06]. The same release includes $152 million of workforce rebalancing charges in both reported and adjusted results.

Study legacy contract runoff, newly retained scope, transition cost, automation and expected versus realized contract profitability. Falsifier: optimistic projected margin on newly signed work does not appear in later delivery economics. Preserve the issuer's actual adjusted definition; do not automatically remove every restructuring charge or treat expected margin as achieved.

### Capgemini — operating-margin terminology and operating profit require a bridge

The July 30 first-half report presents operating margin of EUR1,506 million and EUR628 million of net other operating expenses, producing operating profit of EUR878 million. It separately describes acquisition contribution, restructuring and organic free cash flow [S07]. An apparently common margin label is not enough for a peer comparison.

Research the conversion from modernization and operations demand into retained profit after transition, workforce adaptation, acquisition integration and financing costs. Hypothesis: process ownership plus implementation can support paid operations work. Falsifier: contracts expand reported revenue but restructuring, lower renewal prices or debt service consume the benefit. Do not annualize first-half cash flow mechanically or call constant-currency growth organic growth.

## 6. Two real arithmetic checks and two original scenario families

### 6.1 Kyndryl period split

Using its rounded disclosed USD billions, quarterly signings growth is `3.9/3.2 - 1 = 21.875%`, while trailing growth is `14.2/18.3 - 1 = -22.4044%` [S06]. Display approximately +21.9% and -22.4%, with rounded-input status. The arithmetic does not create exact unrounded growth or explain cause.

The product should show both periods and their source definitions. A change in direction can reflect base effects, contract timing, duration or underlying demand. It is a research question, not proof of a turn.

### 6.2 Capgemini profit bridge

`EUR1,506m - EUR628m = EUR878m`. Against revenue of EUR12,082m, the two rates are approximately 12.4648% and 7.2670%, consistent with the reported rounded 12.5% and 7.3% [S07]. These are different source measures, not two competing estimates of one metric. A common product field must preserve the bridge and accounting basis.

### 6.3 Automation and who captures the savings — hypothetical, not issuer results

Assume one project initially requires 100 hours billed at $100/hour. Labor costs $60/hour and other project costs are $1,000. Revenue is $10,000; delivery cost is $7,000; contribution is $3,000. These figures exclude corporate overhead, tax, financing and capital charges; contribution is not issuer operating profit.

Automation reduces required hours to 60 and adds $800 of project-specific technology expense. Other project costs remain $1,000. New delivery cost is `60 x 60 + 800 + 1,000 = $5,400`.

| Contract assumption | Revenue | Delivery cost | Project contribution | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Unchanged hourly billing rate, only 60 hours billed | $6,000 | $5,400 | $600 | Customer captures the reduced billed effort; provider needs new scope, pricing or capacity redeployment. |
| Unchanged fixed deliverable price | $10,000 | $5,400 | $4,600 | Provider retains the modeled savings until repricing or other costs intervene. |
| Renewed fixed price of $8,500 | $8,500 | $5,400 | $3,100 | Customer saves $1,500 and provider retains $100 more contribution than before. |

At a price of $8,400, the provider preserves its original $3,000 contribution under these assumptions. A 40% reduction in labor hours therefore does not imply a 40% revenue, profit or fair-value increase. Freed capacity becomes additional revenue only if additional paid demand exists. Do not invent demand elasticity or assume every contract permits hourly savings to be retained.

### 6.4 Identical TCV, different timing — hypothetical

Two $120 million commitments lasting two and five years would imply $60 million and $24 million per year only under a simplifying equal-delivery schedule. Actual ramp, variable scope, termination, currency, renewal and accounting can differ. The scenario demonstrates why total value needs duration and a delivery profile. It does not forecast either commitment's revenue or turn a public contract ceiling into funded work.

## 7. Leading indicators, moat and rerating hypotheses

The research should distinguish three reasons a service business can improve: more valuable work, better capture of the work's price, and lower cost of delivering the same accepted outcome. None is inferred from a single AI label.

Potential early observations include signed incremental scope, funded transformation priorities, mobilization, specialist staffing, client program starts, contract duration, pricing/discount changes and acceptance milestones. Confirmation includes comparable organic revenue, paid utilization, contract contribution, collected cash and renewal. Each observation needs a documented source and sample; public job postings, certifications, partner logos and case studies are biased proxies, not complete demand panels.

The proposed moat questions are specific: does the provider possess relevant process knowledge; reliable integration and delivery; a defensible customer relationship; scarce skills; lawful access to necessary customer context; reusable tools; or an ability to assume and manage risk that a generic tool does not? Then ask what evidence distinguishes a genuine advantage from marketing. A successful technology demonstration is not a renewal, and a staff training count is not productive capacity.

Rerating hypotheses must connect operations with expectations and valuation. A provider can benefit from improving new-scope mix, successful backlog conversion, stable renewal prices, lower execution risk and sustained per-share cash generation. A business can also improve profitability by leaving uneconomic work while its revenue declines. Conversely, apparently strong bookings can disappoint if duration extends, scope is cancelled, pricing weakens, transition costs rise or customer automation reduces the billable base. These are hypotheses for historical and prospective testing, not an empirical ranking supplied by this report.

Keep regime channels distinct: customer discretionary budgets, currency, wage costs, financing conditions, contractual indexation and valuation discount rates. Do not use a blanket services-equals-defensive or AI-services-equals-growth rule. Cross-region labor and revenue exposures are separate; a listing country does not describe either one.
