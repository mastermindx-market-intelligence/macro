## A. Executive verdict

**The idea is worth a tightly bounded test, but the literature does not establish the proposed alpha.** The most defensible current product hypothesis is a **continuation/hold evidence state**, potentially useful for tail-risk discrimination. Higher subsequent return, lower tail risk, and a useful explanation of today's behavior are three different successes; none may substitute for another.

There is credible evidence for industry momentum, firm-specific medium-horizon momentum, information-path effects, heterogeneous transmission of common shocks and liquidity recovery. But the closest opposing work says that attractive downside protection can carry **lower** expected returns; temporarily forced-down stocks may subsequently rebound more; and recent residual strength can reverse rather than continue. The published evidence is not a monotonic law that “the name holding best during a selloff wins afterward.” [E01–E17]

**Residualization is necessary, not sufficient.** A rolling linear market/sector model removes some predictable common movement. It does not remove nonlinear beta, changing exposure, omitted quality/style factors, nonsynchronous prices, contemporaneous stock-specific news, or selection bias. It also does not identify who is buying. [E03–E05, E09–E13]

**The retained data do not currently admit the proposed non-leaky historical sector study.** The inspected S&P1500 “PIT sectors” collector explicitly supplies current classifications, and its receipt contains **zero era-correct sector labels**. The historical membership file is real, but PIT membership is not PIT sector classification. The issue is not that historical prices are absent. [M16; SOURCE_DATA_AUDIT]

A new outcome-blind census found **1,837 onsets across 409 names in 2014–2025**, of which **915 have a nonblank sector** and **922 do not**. These counts precede security-type, historical classification, leader persistence, first-shock, factor-history and label-quality filters. They are not SLR event counts. The complete retained parent has 2,650 rows, not 2,650 independent, qualified equity challenges. [SOURCE_DATA_AUDIT]

**Decision:** retain SLR-P0 as a falsifiable research proposal; freeze the exact development specification; do not run a current-sector backtest and call it non-leaky; do not introduce production authority; do not buy L3 or build another collector as the default response. First resolve historical classification/identity through existing owners and establish whether enough independent challenges remain to justify the experiment.

## C. External literature synthesis

### Industry versus firm-specific momentum

**PUBLISHED — Moskowitz and Grinblatt** show that industry movement explains an important part of stock momentum. The relevant evidence is portfolio formation/holding at monthly horizons, not a first-shock daily event. **Implication:** compare names exposed to the same environment and distinguish common industry continuation from stock-specific response. [E01]

**PUBLISHED — Blitz, Huij and Martens** construct residual momentum from rolling factor regressions and lagged standardized residual formation returns. **Gutierrez and Pirinsky** distinguish relative-return momentum from firm-specific abnormal-return behavior; the latter source was accessed at abstract/author-summary level, not replicated. Neither establishes that a single unusually positive shock residual predicts a further month of leadership. [E02, E03]

**PUBLISHED — there is a live attribution dispute.** Ehsani and Linnainmaa attribute stock momentum to factor momentum. Graef, Höchle and Schmid's 2025 study finds a stronger role for firm-specific momentum under alternative decompositions. Its medium-term and short-term findings differ: the latest residual-return component exhibits reversal. Thus “residual” is not synonymous with “continue buying.” [E04, E05]

### Market state, path quality and defensive characteristics

**PUBLISHED — Cooper, Gutierrez and Hameed** find market-state dependence in momentum. **Daniel and Moskowitz** analyze panic states and loser-rebound exposure in momentum crashes. The relevant falsifier is not that a leader must lose money absolutely; it can lose its relative advantage when highly exposed prior losers rebound. A future rebound cannot be used as an ex-ante crisis label. [E06, E08]

**PUBLISHED — Frog-in-the-Pan** links information continuity to momentum. Its information-discreteness variable counts return signs; it is not a general geometric smoothness or volatility measure. Consequently SLR must control both the published sign-path variable and ordinary volatility/path shape rather than use “smoothness” as a loose synonym. The familiar returns reported in that paper must not be confused across monthly and multi-month holding periods. [E07]

**PUBLISHED — downside risk is not a settled one-sign story.** Ang, Chen and Xing report compensation for downside exposure. Betting Against Beta studies leverage-constrained demand and risk-adjusted low-beta performance, not a universal promise of higher unlevered returns from every defensive stock. Wang challenges a prominent downside-risk-premium interpretation and shows why predictive bear betas need not deliver reliable realized protection. [E09–E11]

**PUBLISHED — the closest adverse evidence comes from realized semibetas.** Bollerslev, Patton and Quaedvlieg distinguish negative-stock/negative-market comovement from positive-stock/negative-market comovement. The latter defensive component predicts lower, not higher, subsequent returns in their setting. This is not a first-challenge leader study, but it directly prevents treating attractive shock behavior as automatically positive alpha. [E12]

Their author-listed 2026 forthcoming **Granular Betas** paper, inspected in its May 2025 version, generalizes conditional exposure across the factor-return distribution. It uses long daily histories and monthly cross-sectional pricing, not the proposed 21-day leader test. **Inference:** a simple linear residual may still contain nonlinear risk exposure; do not build another factor engine before testing a modest conditional-beta baseline. [E13]

### Common shocks: persistence is not drift

**PUBLISHED — financial-flexibility research around COVID** links pre-crisis balance-sheet differences to relative crisis resilience. But a price gap that remains after a common shock is not the same as additional abnormal return earned after the resilience observation becomes known. A valid SLR label must begin after that observation. [E14]

**PUBLISHED — Cella, Ellul and Giannetti** find that short-horizon institutional ownership amplifies common-shock price declines and subsequent reversals. **Coval and Stafford** link observed fund pressure to fire-sale effects. These mechanisms can predict more rebound in the stocks that initially fell further—the opposite of a universal “held best, wins next” rule. They use investor/holding information, not an OHLCV forced-selling classifier. [E15, E16]

**PUBLISHED — Demers and coauthors** show how a seemingly protective characteristic can lose its interpretation after risk, industry and accounting controls. The lesson is not to import their particular factor, but to resist naming a residual “structural quality” before the competing exposures are tested. [E17]

### Liquidity, order flow and identifiable absorption

**PUBLISHED — Chordia, Roll and Subrahmanyam** document common liquidity variation, and **Chordia and Subrahmanyam** model autocorrelated imbalances and order splitting. The sign of a lagged-imbalance relation changes when contemporaneous imbalance is included. Return/flow timing and inventory accommodation matter; an intuitive flow story alone is not an identified forecast. [E18, E19]

**PUBLISHED — Anand and coauthors** use institution-specific trading data to study recovery in trading costs. Their resiliency outcome is liquidity recovery, not next-month residual stock return. Institution-level supply/withdrawal is informative about that outcome, but it cannot be recovered merely by seeing a large print or a green volume bar. [E20]

**PUBLISHED — Caught on Tape** estimates institutional activity using a mapping to quarterly ownership information; its conclusions differ from a simple large-trade cutoff. The accessible institutional repository summary supports that distinction; the full PDF fetch failed, so this report does not claim a full-method replication. [E21]

**PUBLISHED — Cont, Kukanov and Stoikov** relate contemporaneous price changes to quote-event order-flow imbalance and available depth. Some OFI construction mechanically includes price-changing events. High contemporaneous fit is not a 21-day forecast, participant identification, or proof that an apparent absorber will keep supporting the price. [E22]

### Recent checks through 2026

The November 2025 Federal Reserve Treasury study reinforces the interaction of imbalance and depth in stress episodes, but it is a different asset class and horizon. The March 2026 spoofing study explicitly cannot identify a common investor behind every candidate order/trade pattern without investor identifiers. Both are useful limits on interpretation, not US-equity SLR validation. The 2025 residual-momentum challenge and 2026-listed granular-beta work materially changed this study's falsifiers. Sources with uncertain publication timing or only secondary AI summaries were not promoted into evidence. [E05, E13, E23, E24]

**Synthesis:** credible mechanisms exist, but the retrieved literature does not directly establish the exact conditional Detector-D → first common shock → unexpected resilience → remaining 21-session sector-relative return estimand. This is a reason to test it carefully, not a claim that no relevant paper can exist.

## D. Strongest support for the thesis

**INFERENCE:** the credible positive case is a *conditional information update*. A known leader has already demonstrated medium-horizon demand. An external challenge provides a new stress observation. A response beyond calibrated common exposure may reveal a persistent firm-specific component omitted from prior momentum. If investors adjust slowly, the update could continue; if latent support protects future price paths, it could lower tail risk without increasing the mean. Residual-momentum, information-path and liquidity-recovery studies make these mechanisms plausible, but do not validate this chain. [E02, E05, E07, E20]

The two-landmark extension is conceptually useful because it asks whether a stock that resisted adverse conditions also participates in an independently observed rebound. It separates a defensive shelter from a more symmetric or convex participant **at the later decision time**. Its incremental predictive value still has to be measured.

## E. Strongest case against the thesis

**INFERENCE:** six explanations can produce attractive charts without new alpha: low or asymmetric exposure; recent residual continuation/reversal already captured by incumbent features; stale or nonsynchronous marks; coincident firm news; common-shock selection driven by industry/era composition; and a defensive characteristic whose protection is already priced.

An additional construction trap is mathematical: once raw return, factors and loadings are known, the residual is a transformation of those inputs. It is not a new independent sensor. A flexible baseline containing the same information may match it. The scientific novelty must come from **conditional predictive structure**, not from a different label for subtraction.

**DERIVED — an even more specific false-positive mechanism was caught before the protocol freeze.** If both the challenge residual and future residual label subtract the same noisy fitted mean, their errors are positively correlated even when true future returns are independent. For H future sessions and estimation error delta, that covariance is H×Var(delta). Under a simple IID null with 200 training observations and 2% daily innovation volatility, the apparent slope on a one-unit standardized challenge residual is approximately **0.209 percentage points over 21 sessions** without any predictive signal. This is analytical null arithmetic, not a market estimate. The final primary therefore uses coefficient-free peer-relative future return; shared-model residual outcomes are secondary and cannot rescue a failed primary. [METHODS_DERIVATION.json]

The strongest adversarial test is therefore: does the same residual innovation become more predictive specifically during an independently measured shock, after pre-shock risk/path/momentum controls? If not, SLR may be a useful way to explain an ordinary residual update, but it has not established a distinct shock-resilience phenomenon.

## F. Formal mechanism model

A useful decomposition is:

`observed return = predictable common exposure + firm-news response + liquidity/inventory effects + measurement error`.

The estimated challenge residual still contains every part of that expression not explained by the fitted factor model. Hidden demand is only one possible contributor. Prior leadership, firm quality, volatility, owner horizon and liquidity can affect both the challenge response and future outcomes. Conditioning on having remained a leader can also select a special survivor state. None of these variables is randomly assigned.

**Illustration, not market evidence:** a −4% market move with ordinary beta 1 predicts −4%. A stock with a true downside beta of 0.4 falls only −1.6%, producing an apparent +2.4-point residual. If its upside beta is also 0.4, a later +6% rebound produces only +2.4%, not the +6% implied by the same ordinary-beta ruler. The initial “strength” requires no latent alpha at all. Conversely, a stock with stronger upside participation could look identical at the first landmark. [Derived algebra; competing-exposure motivation E09–E13]

The research can estimate conditional associations and compare out-of-time predictive representations. It cannot infer the causal effect of an unobserved institution choosing to absorb selling. Such a claim would need independent participant/flow identification and a different design.

## H. Exact SLR-P0 design and the choices made

The separate frozen protocol is controlling for execution. Its central choices are:

**Population:** canonical Detector-D onsets; first qualified common challenge in D+21..D+63; only then require incumbent continuation at C−1. One onset, one challenge. A failed first eligibility check is not a search for another challenge. +21 is a deliberate seasoning interval, not an optimal threshold inferred from returns. Formal Leader Radar leadership is a separate qualified descriptive subset.

**Shock:** at least 20 other issuers in the historically valid same-sector basket, selected on lagged type/liquidity criteria; equal-weight negative peer return with lagged z at most −1 and at least 60% negative breadth. No subject listings, thin-sector fallback, missing-name reweighting or current-sector substitution. The subject's return never chooses the challenge.

**Response:** compare raw return, simple peer-relative return, joint lagged market/orthogonal-sector residual, and residual z. Fit the exact preceding window through C−1. Preserve the existing owners but use an isolated research adapter for the specified math.

**Primary endpoint:** 21-session stock total return minus the equal-initial-weight return of C-frozen ex-self peers. It contains no fitted coefficients. The proposed fitted-residual primary was rejected before freeze because shared estimation error can manufacture predictability. Fitted residual sums at 5/21/63 and peer-relative return at 63 are secondary diagnostics, not alternative promotion routes. Future tail metrics exclude the challenge candle. A single 21-session endpoint is a pragmatic tactical choice; no paper proves it is optimal.

**Best counterfactual:** other qualified prior leaders sharing the same challenge date and historical sector. This is closer than unchallenged stocks or retrospectively selected winners. It costs sample size: singleton cells cannot identify a within-shock slope and must be disclosed.

**New falsifier:** at an independent scheduled D+21 landmark, test whether the residual×common-shock interaction adds anything beyond generic residual innovation. This tests the special stress-state story rather than only showing that residual returns contain information.

## I. Statistical design and realistic power

Ticker-row IID inference is not appropriate: many stocks experience the same shock, issuers repeat, and 21/63-session outcomes overlap. The protocol therefore specifies within-shock effects, issuer/date cluster intervals, a full-cross-section time-block interval, purged annual chronological evaluation and no random-row cross-validation. Neither clustering nor a bootstrap makes a small number of crises into a large sample. [E27, E28]

One primary test is separated from a fixed 14-test secondary family, corrected with Holm. Robustness subsets are not another menu of uncounted discoveries. Existing consumed history and the factor-research multiplicity problem mean even a positive historical result can justify only a future confirmation proposal. [E29]

**DERIVED power illustration, not observed SLR precision:** with a hypothetical 21-session outcome standard deviation of 8%, a one-unit residualized Z variance of one, and 915 independent observations, the normal-approximation 80%-power detectable slope is about **0.74 percentage points**. The corresponding ideal effective sample size for a 0.50-point slope is **2,008**; with 10% outcome volatility it is **3,136**. Filters, controls and dependence generally make the information requirement harder, not easier. [POWER_ILLUSTRATION.json]

These assumptions are not measured SLR volatility or incidence. Actual event counts, independent shock dates, effect variance and achievable precision are unknown because the primary data are not admitted and labels remain closed. Tail-risk tests may be even weaker when severe events are rare. The practical response is to audit incidence and precision before commissioning a large rebuild—not to lower significance hurdles.

## L. Robustness and falsification

A future admitted run must show every requested result, including null and adverse outcomes. The key challenges are conditional-beta attenuation; short-reversal dominance; ordinary residual-update equivalence; disappearance outside technology or a handful of dates; source/gap/delisting dependence; shock-specific versus generic path effects; and failure during panic rebounds. Multiple specifications cannot be searched until one becomes profitable.

A relative tail benefit must be evaluated with close-based future MAE and severe-tail probability, not with how little the stock already fell on C. Lower contemporaneous loss is part of the feature and cannot validate itself as a future outcome. Shared-model residual-label error can manufacture return predictability; source-specific zero returns or excluded bankruptcies can manufacture apparent tail protection.

At landmark B, report the entire C cohort's follow-up accounting, including no rebound, unobservable first rebound and terminal events. Conditioning retrospectively on names that later participated in a rebound and assigning their subsequent gains to C is immortal-time/selection leakage. The B estimator begins after B and answers a later question.

## N. Microstructure Phase 2: architecture, not overclaim

**Named capability:** distinguish an unusually small price concession under measurable adverse flow from a simple low-beta move, and evaluate whether that distinction improves a subsequent continuation/tail forecast. Select the cohort from the **same frozen price-defined challenge**, not by retrospectively spotting attractive flow patterns.

**L1** needs continuous, time-ordered best bid/ask prices and sizes plus trades, conditions/corrections, spread, quote age and sequence/coverage receipts. Trade-signed volume and quote-event OFI are different measurements. Trade-attached BBO snapshots do not reconstruct all intervening adds/cancels or reliable replenishment. Use lag-trained expected impact conditioned on flow, depth and time of day; do not divide by near-zero OFI and call the unstable ratio hidden demand. [E19, E22, M17]

**L2** can add multi-level depth slope, convexity, depletion and restoration. It earns its cost only if those variables improve out-of-time prediction or execution-risk discrimination beyond L1. Snapshot frequency, missed messages and venue coverage remain part of the evidence vector, not invisible engineering details.

**L3/MBO** can reconstruct displayed order lifetimes and add/cancel/execute/replacement behavior when sequence integrity is intact. Nasdaq's documented order reference and optional MPID attribution do not identify every beneficial owner, strategy, parent metaorder or motive. Even sophisticated 2026 manipulation-pattern research states identity limitations. Replenishment may be consistent with reserve liquidity or multiple independent participants; label it an inference, not proof of an institutional campaign. [E24, E26]

**FINRA short-marked volume is not short interest, trade aggressor direction, bearish intent, institutional buying or net dealer inventory.** A large print, off-exchange print, wick, “green volume,” or low price impact alone cannot establish institutional absorption. Call a qualified conjunction an **absorption candidate** and retain alternative explanations. [E21, E25; M03]
